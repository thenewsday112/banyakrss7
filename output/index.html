I used an airline hack to turn a layover into a mini trip to Iceland. It was worth the short detour — and saved me money.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
I used an airline travel hack to turn a layover into a mini-vacation and save on airfare.

Sergei Gapon/Anadolu Agency via Getty Images, Gentry Hale

  • I booked an airline "stopover" to visit Iceland for 4 days on my way home from Norway.
  • The ticket was one itinerary and didn't cost extra airfare, but included no free hotel or tours.
  • The mini-trip was expensive, despite the shoulder season, but it's an easy way to see a new country.

After five fabulous days exploring Norway's stunning fjords in mid-September, I was excited to try an airline travel hack I'd been eyeing for months: Icelandair's "stopover" program.

Icelandair allows passengers traveling between North America and Europe to stop in Iceland for up to 7 nights on a standard ticket without paying additional airfare. Higher-priced flex tickets allow up to 21 nights.

I chose a $455 one-way standard economy flight from Norway to the US with a four-day stop. All of Icelandair's transatlantic routes already pass through Keflavík International Airport near Reykjavík, making it an easy detour.

This strategy effectively added a second vacation onto my trip. I soaked in geothermal water amid moody volcanic landscapes, ate my way through Reykjavík, and explored part of the south coast.

The stopover was worth it, and I felt I had enough time to explore, but it was not a free vacation.

Iceland is expensive, even during the shoulder season, and while the airline doesn't charge extra for the stopover, it does not cover accommodations, tours, or transfers.

Still, the travel hack — which about a dozen other global airlines offer — let me visit a remote country that had been at the top of my list for years without having to book a separate trip across the Atlantic.

Booking my 4-day stopover was surprisingly simple.

Entering Iceland.

Gentry Hale

Icelandair's booking engine has a dedicated stopover option. I entered Bergen, Norway, as my origin and Portland, Oregon, as my final destination, chose the dates I wanted to arrive and depart Iceland, and booked both flights on a single itinerary.

I stopped in Iceland on my way home from Norway because the logistics and airfare worked out better than they had on my outbound trip. Flight prices can vary widely by day of the week, so it's worth comparing a few departure dates before booking.

The flight from Bergen to Iceland was two and a half hours, and the flight from Iceland to Oregon was about eight hours. Icelandair's economy service was very basic and felt sparse for an international flight. Water, coffee, and tea were complimentary, though meals and snacks cost extra.

Saga Premium, the airline's highest-tier cabin, was a marked step up, with a wider recliner seat, more legroom, meals, WiFi, and complimentary drinks. It looked far more comfortable but came with a significant price jump — up to $2,000 or more one-way, depending on the date.

Four days gave me time to see more of the country.

A buggy ride on a black sand beach.

Gentry Hale

Booking a few days in Iceland proved to be key to enjoying the stopover. Staying only one night would probably be more effort than it is worth.

Keflavík Airport is roughly a 45-minute drive from Reykjavík, so an overnight stop might amount to one of Iceland's iconic lagoon soaks, then dinner, sleep, and another trip down the same highway.

Two or three nights is ideal if you want to get a feel for Reykjavík. And the freshly baked cinnamon rolls at Brauð & Co are arguably reason enough to add a few-day stopover.

I found that staying for four or more days let me split my trip, spending a few days in Reykjavík and a few days exploring deeper into the desolate, elemental country.

The stopover does not include a hotel or activity discounts.

The Reykjavík EDITION Hotel.

Gentry Hale

Icelandair does not include a stopover hotel, and passengers must arrange that separately, but they are eligible to book tours and transfers through the airline.

Some other airline stopover programs do offer passengers free overnight accommodations.

For example, Turkish Airlines stopover passengers can select from specific comped hotels, Etihad Airways offers eligible travelers up to two complimentary nights in Abu Dhabi or Al Ain, and Qatar Airways sells Doha stopover packages, including hotel stays starting at $14 per person.

However, the stopover hotels are sometimes far from the city's main attractions, and passengers are often better off booking on their own based on what they want to do.

I found that not having a set hotel gave me the freedom to explore.

I spent two nights at The Reykjavík EDITION, a 5-star luxury hotel, for around $800 a night. My room had harbor views and put me within easy reach of the city's main attractions. Reykjavík also offers more affordable hotels, guesthouses, and hostels for budget travelers.

I also stayed a few nights on the south coast, where people can do more adventurous activities.

You will need a plan to get beyond Reykjavík.

A stop along the south coast.

Gentry Hale

Iceland looks compact on a map, but its best-known sights are scattered hours from the capital city, and there are no passenger trains.

Bus service reaches many towns, but travelers who want to efficiently reach sights beyond Reykjavík will likely want to rent a car, hire a driver, or join a tour.

We drove down to the south coast to see beyond the city life. We stayed at a boutique hotel called Hotel Rangá, near Hella, about 60 miles from Reykjavík. It was around $650 a night, but we had plenty of hot tubs and direct access to some of Iceland's best outdoor adventures.

While we pieced together our own trip, passengers can also book excursions, like horseback riding, whale-watching, lava tunnel adventures, and a Golden Circle and lagoon day, through Icelandair.

However, booking through the airline doesn't necessarily mean getting a discount, so it's worth comparing prices with individual tour operators before reserving.

More active travelers can join guided glacier hikes or ice-cave tours, or bounce across black sand in a buggy.

For me, the stopover was worth it

The Seljalandsfoss waterfall.

Gentry Hale

Michael Raucheisen, Icelandair's North American communications manager, told me the airline formally began promoting stopovers in the 1960s.

The point was not simply to make connections less tedious, but to expose travelers to Iceland and encourage them to return: "That's kind of the goal, to give you a small little taste to make you want to come back," Raucheisen said.

It worked on me.

Iceland is unlike any of the dozens of other countries I've visited, from its moody volcanic vistas and geothermal activity to its down-to-earth people, world-class hot dogs, and the country's nose-wrinkling national drink, Brennivín.

The Reykjaböð Hot Springs, on Iceland's southwest coast.

Gentry Hale

But Iceland is not a cheap country to visit. While traveling with another person helped us split the hotel costs, I estimate my share of our four-day trip was about $510 per day, or roughly $2,050 total, excluding airfare.

That includes hotel nights and typical daily costs for food, sightseeing, and local transportation. Travelers could likely lower that cost by choosing less expensive lodging.

Still, though I may not do it as expensively every time, I look forward to adding a few nights in Iceland whenever the route and fare make sense — it's a chance to see more of the country without paying for a whole separate trip.

My version of a marathon is a 32-mile walk around Manhattan. It's a great way to see NYC.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
The author (right) said she has never run a marathon, but has walked the perimeter of Manhattan, around 32 miles, several times.

Courtesy of Vanessa Ogle.

  • I haven't run a marathon or a half-marathon.
  • But I have walked the perimeter of Manhattan, around 32 miles, three times.
  • The Great Saunter is an annual walk that I enjoy immensely and plan to do again.

I haven't run a marathon. I haven't even run a half-marathon. The longest race I've run is a 5k. But earlier this year, I walked 32 miles — the perimeter of Manhattan — for the third time.

This iconic New York City walk, called the Great Saunter, is held annually during the first week of May. This year, I walked with friends. It took about 12 hours, and even though I wore brand-new sneakers (which is usually ill-advised, since you want to break in new shoes), I recovered quickly and was out and about the next day.

The walk was a great opportunity to catch up with friends, see places around New York City that I don't usually visit, and partake in one of the unique activities that make this city so special.

The walk has some history

The Great Saunter began when the Shorewalkers' founder, Cy A Adler, who passed away in 2018, walked the route in 1982. What started as a fun and informal walk has grown into an event that drew more than 3,000 walkers who took on the challenge of completing the entire route, which runs from the West Side of Manhattan to the East Side.

Not everyone completes the full walk. Some people join for the beginning or vow to make it to the halfway point. Sometimes people start, then head to the finish line to wait for friends who were planning to complete the entire course.

This year, Shorewalkers reported that around 62% of walkers finished the event.

The route was a unique way to see the city

Even though I've lived in New York City for more than a decade, the longer-than-a-marathon walk around Manhattan offers such a special way to see the city. Usually, I take the subway to get to and from places, so it was wonderful to explore the city's perimeter in full, especially in the lovely spring weather.

Though the morning started out rainy, the rest of the day was perfect for walking. By the time my friends and I started walking at around 7:30 a.m., the weather was clear and crisp.

The author said the course of the Great Saunter took her past many iconic spots of the NYC shoreline, including The Little Red Lighthouse beneath the George Washington Bridge.

Courtesy of Vanessa Ogle.

I loved walking along the West Side Highway and seeing all the flowers and little parks along the way, like the iconic Little Island — a floating park — which we passed by early on in our journey, as well as The Little Red Lighthouse, located in Fort Washington Park, with its vividness that really makes it stand out against the city landscape.

For lunch, we stopped at Inwood Hill Park, which was about halfway. Then, we headed down to the East Side.

What I did differently this time around to finish quicker

This year, even with bathroom breaks and lunch, I finished the walk in a little over 12 hours. The other two years took longer, and those walks also resulted in more soreness.

I wanted to finish quicker this year so I could spend less time on my feet overall. To do that, I really minimized breaks. I allowed myself to sit down a few times, but I resisted the urge to get too comfortable.

Also, this was the first year that my friends were able to do the route with me. When I completed it the first time, I ran into people I knew and made friends along the way. But in 2024, I completed it entirely on my own, which was much more mentally taxing.

This year, it was great to chat with friends along the way, and it definitely made the time go by much more quickly.

I'm looking forward to walking again in 2027 and will be ready when registration opens in February. Until then, I'm staying on my feet and taking in all the museums, parks, and — of course — the beautiful walking paths the city offers.

I finished my holiday shopping in October. I saved money by shopping at Dollar Tree, thrift stores, and on clearance racks.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
The author, shown shopping at Dollar Tree, has been stashing away inexpensive holiday gifts throughout the year.

Courtesy of Ashley Archambault.

  • Every December, I spend more than I expect to on holiday gifts, and shopping takes up so much time.
  • This year, I shopped early at thrift stores and from the clearance racks at stores I love.
  • I finished my shopping in October and will have more time to spend with my family in December.

I love the atmosphere of stores during the Christmas season. The decorations, the elaborate displays, the gift boxes. I enjoy it all. But I usually catch myself spending more than I intended.

This year, I wanted to avoid that. So, instead of a mad dash to get everything done in the weeks between Thanksgiving and Christmas, I've been picking up things throughout the year for my loved ones.

This strategy of buying one or two low-cost things here and there, instead of buying a lot at once in December, has been way easier on my budget, too.

Much to my surprise, I was completely done with my shopping in October. Now I'll be free to enjoy the holiday season with my friends and family instead of stressing about getting gifts for everyone on my list.

I embraced thrifting

At the beginning of the year, I started thinking a lot about what my money might be supporting when I decided to shop at a particular store. I wanted to be more conscientious of where I was spending my money, especially when my budget feels tight due to rising costs.

The author (center), shown with her family last Christmas, said she has spent December running around looking for gifts in past years.

Courtesy of Ashley Archambault.

I started going to thrift stores that directly support a charity, so I feel like I'm finding treasures, keeping them out of the landfill, and helping a cause I love. At thrift stores, I primarily find clothes, kitchenware, home decor, and vintage or analog things, like CDs, DVDs, and craft supplies. I have found thoughtful gifts for almost everyone I know.

I found good deals in an unexpected place

Our family also celebrates Jólabókaflóð, an Icelandic holiday centered on gifting books, which we exchange on Christmas Eve.

I've acquired so many books for my husband and son from used book sales at my local libraries that I'll be able to give them each a book-of-the-month box for 2027.

My local library feels like a well-kept secret when it comes to buying used books. I often leave with a bag full of new hardcover bestsellers, children's books, and even cookbooks for under $5 — total. I love that these sales directly support the library and help me stay out of trouble with my own book budget.

I stocked up without a plan

I've always loved Trader Joe's and Aldi, but when I discovered there was a whole beauty-dupe scene at these stores, it became a hobby to peruse their shelves for fun. I've picked up several self-care items throughout the year that I know will be perfect for someone on my list — maybe a teacher or a girlfriend — because at these lower prices, I can afford to buy extra things to keep on hand.

I've had the most luck finding amazing deals on Christmas gifts within the clearance aisles at Marshalls and TJ Maxx. I've always loved both stores, but I challenged myself on my visits to only shop from the clearance sections for steals. I got a little addicted to the thrill of finding something high quality at a bargain price. On one trip to Marshall's, I found a beautiful pair of pearl earrings for my aunt and a sweater set for a good friend. Each gift was under $10.

The author, shown with her son when he was 1, said she is looking forward to spending more time with her family this holiday season.

Courtesy of Ashley Archambault.

I've also grabbed a few stocking stuffers every time I've been to Dollar Tree or Five Below. I know I've scored things my son will enjoy come Christmas morning.

This December, I'm giving myself time back

Past holidays have been so busy that I don't always get to do the things I actually want to do. For example, I love making salt dough ornaments, but haven't had time to do so in years. I also love making cookies while watching classic movies or driving around with my family to see holiday lights. Now, I can do these kinds of things more than just once this year. I can't wait.

This new indie film about an AI startup founder and his family feels less like sci-fi by the day

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
"Anna by the Window" is a film co-directed by filmmakers Myles Flusser and James Hyland.

BaggySuit

  • "Anna by the Window" is a coming-of-age film by filmmakers Myles Flusser and James Hyland.
  • The film explores grief and human connection in the age of generative AI.
  • Flusser said the film was at first a "speculative sci-fi" about AI, but then fiction became reality.

From tech behemoths scrambling to replicate OpenAI's success with ChatGPT to Twitter rebranding to X under Elon Musk, Silicon Valley was undergoing an undeniable transformation in 2023.

Nearly 3,000 miles away in New York, two skeptical 20-somethings were trying to make sense of it all. So, they made a film.

"It was very personal," James Hyland, 25, told Business Insider. "We wanted to put something together that spoke to the things we were going through and what the world was like. That ended up being artificial intelligence and technological alienation."

Hyland and Myles Flusser created "Anna by the Window," a coming-of-age film exploring grief and how technology can stifle human connection in the era of generative AI. The independent filmmakers wrote, directed, and produced the film in 2023 alongside their friends under their production company, Baggysuit.

"Anna by the Window" is a coming-of-age film exploring grief and human connection in the digital era.

Baggysuit

"Particularly, it feels like the people running these platforms — YouTube, Instagram, et cetera — seem more contented than ever in letting it become a total slop apocalypse full of AI bots and non-human content," Flusser, 26, said. "That intrigues me in a way and scares me, obviously."

Zoomers like Flusser and Hyland grew up inundated with smartphones, social media, and all the risks that come along with them. A year after they filmed "Anna by the Window," the US Surgeon General said social media should have a warning label because of its impact on mental health. Earlier this year, Meta settled a lawsuit brought by a coalition of states accusing it of designing platforms to addict children.

The din of constant digital exposure has led some young folks to join the neo-Luddite movement, which takes a stand against overreliance on modern technology by emphasizing IRL human connection.

Hyland is an organizer for a Luddite group called The Lamp Club. While the film was not made because of the Luddite movement, it shares many of its themes.

"I think that it speaks to the synergy of our separate film project and this larger organizing project that's materializing all over the country, where people are fed up with these kinds of technological systems," Hyland said.

When fiction becomes reality

The "Anna by the Window" crew began screening the film in July.

Baggysuit

"Anna by the Window" is a glimpse inside the world of Ryan, a teenage boy who travels to New York City to reconnect with his older brother, James, the founder of an AI startup.

The film's crew shot across New York State and used a variety of tools to bring the project to life, including ElevenLabs AI audio software to create the voice of Ryan and James' father throughout the film. The pair also pushed a real ChatGPT chatbot toward hallucination and included that audio in the film.

"There's an AI chatbot that goes off the rails, you could say. We really cranked up all the dials," Hyland said. "It started saying 'error.' It started speaking different languages."

Flusser said they wanted to portray the eerie nature of AI just right. They're not eager to use AI products in future projects, though. The majority of the film relies on traditional tools like a Sony camera, written outlines, and improv.

"There's a strangeness and alienation that come up when we interact with chatbots and AI. We were trying to capture that feeling as purely as possible, so it felt totally necessary to use AI to do that," Flusser said.

James Hyland and Myles Flusser used Eleven Labs and ChatGPT to create parts of the film.

Baggysuit

While aspects of the film are fictional, others pull directly from the directors' lives.

"My brother had recently died. There's a lot of stuff in there that has to do with that," Flusser said. "There is a climactic moment between the two brothers in this movie that is actually surgically removed from my own life and recreated on screen."

However, the way the filmmakers chose to characterize James in the film — delusional, stunted, running from reality — is distinct from Flusser's real brother. In the film, James is portrayed by Hyland, who said his version of an AI startup founder isn't based on a specific person.

"My character was in a state of denial. Using this technology to be the thing that could deliver him closure or be the thing that he could connect with," Hyland said. "It makes him, in a way, kind of a sympathetic character."

'Anna by the Window' on the silver screen

Hollywood is fixated on the real lives of tech founders. Production companies have backed films about SpaceX founder Elon Musk, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg, and former Theranos CEO Elizabeth Holmes. Even "Toy Story 5" tackled tech by introducing a smart tablet as the story's antagonist.

"Anna by the Window" now joins the list of films putting the tech industry under a harsh microscope.

"When we first started filming this, we were more mystified by AI, but toward the end our relationship with AI felt very cynical," Flusser said.

"Anna by the Window" will become available to stream on Dolph on December 15.

Baggysuit

Watching AI evolve while creating a film about the technology was surreal, Hyland and Flusser said.

"It was strange. When we were making it, we saw this movie as a speculative sci-fi about artificial intelligence," Flusser said. "Now, it's like nothing really is sci-fi about it anymore."

The "Anna by the Window" crew began screening the film this summer in New York City and the Hudson Valley. There are upcoming screenings in Los Angeles this November. The film becomes available on Dolph, a streaming platform, beginning December 15.

Vibe coding is old news. People are 'vibe manufacturing' now.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
David Plakon prototyped an answering box for his kids with GPT-6 Astra.

David Plakon

  • These techies are using AI to prototype answering machines, toys, and jewelry.
  • They call it "vibe manufacturing," a play on the term vibe coding.
  • The vibe manufacturers are bullish on its potential. "AI literally does replace CAD engineering completely," one said.

Jeff Deehan is a real estate developer by day and a "hobby guy" by night.

The 42-year-old has a workshop in his East Lansing house. One project remained elusive: a small piece of steel to secure a camper to his truck. While Deehan was toying with OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra in voice mode, he described the piece he needed and the angle at which it would sit. Five minutes later, the chatbot had prototyped it.

GPT-6 Astra designed a steel part to secure a camper to Jeff Deehan's truck.

Jeff Deehan

Deehan told GPT-6 that he knew of a company called SendCutSend that does custom sheet-metal fabrication. The chatbot downloaded its design as a DXF file, opened SendCutSend's website, filled in the form, and paid the $156 with the payment information he'd stored. Ten days later, it was on his doorstep.

"In about 10 minutes, I spoke a part out of my mind into existence, without touching my phone," he said.

Deehan is part of a growing class of enthusiasts who are building physical gizmos with AI's help. They use chatbots to build prototypes, then send the AI-generated files to manufacturers, boutique fabricators, and their home 3D printers.

Jeff Deehan ordered the truck piece from SendCutSend. It shipped in 10 days.

Jeff Deehan

Modeling physical items with AI has its risks. Deehan was sure his prototype would be a millimeter off here or there, though it ended up being right on. Other vibe manufacturers said that their products took multiple iterations, burning through their budgets.

Normies have long used AI to write software, now dubbed "vibe coding." These builders say AI is coming for hardware creation, too — and they're calling it "vibe manufacturing."

A growing CADtastrophe

Amy Zhou and Jake Fitzgerald are building an open-source tool for vibe manufacturing. They call it "text-to-CAD" — and it already tops 23,000 stars on GitHub.

Zhou previously worked on the iPhone cases team at Apple and is now a design engineer at the AI rendering startup Vizcom. Fitzgerald wanted to get back into robotics after pivoting to crypto. They text new ideas back and forth between San Francisco and New York.

The duo hopes to upend CAD, or computer-aided design. CAD engineers use software to design and optimize 3D models. It takes training and skill. The vibe manufacturers say you can do it easily with a large language model.

Zhou makes jewelry with AI. The 25-year-old's previous startup turned pictures users uploaded into 3D models of charms, then cast the models in wax. It made a necklace for Cluely's CEO, Roy Lee, before Zhou left the business. She still occasionally makes AI-rendered charms for friends when they want necklaces of their pets.

Amy Zhou turns photos into wax-cast jewelry with AI's help.

Amy Zhou

Fitzgerald is more interested in making robot arms. Using the text-to-CAD tool, the 31-year-old designed the arm in sheet metal and paid about $200 to have it built by SendCutSend. It has 0.6 meters of reach, with a motor and a camera, and he considers it an "edutainment" product to learn about robotics before the industry booms.

A few months ago, Fitzgerald saw "compounding failures" when the AI would build bad designs on top of bad designs. The screw holes came in loose, for example. He spent about $2,000 repeatedly ordering pieces. With GPT-6 Astra, he said that's no longer a problem.

Jake Fitzgerald prototyped a robot arm with his text-to-CAD AI tool.

Jake Fitzgerald

Sourish Sharma has watched AI change CAD. The 20-year-old from London got an MRI scan for a research study three years ago and asked for his data. He got a 3D printer and used an open-source tool to turn the data file into something printable. The tool didn't work very well, so he had to edit it by hand. "Some of the folds of the brain were not very foldy," he said.

Now, Sharma has a much bigger printer — and AI. With one prompt, GPT-6 Astra blew the open-source tool out of the water, converting the data and making what he considers a more accurate brain. He's printed out a life-size one, as well as a handful of miniatures that he gives to friends.

Sourish Sharma used AI to turn data from his MRI into a 3D-printed brain.

Sourish Sharma

"AI literally does replace CAD engineering completely," Sharma said. "It's kind of unfortunate if you're a CAD engineer. Fortunately, I'm not."

The first audience: kids

The more complex a product is, the more difficult it is to vibe manufacture. You won't be able to make a car at home with ChatGPT anytime soon.

There's one population whose products are often simple and accessible: kids. Arthur Prince, a 34-year-old IT worker from outside Paris, said the children's toy he modeled in a week with AI would've taken him months with his CAD knowledge alone.

If you don't like the look of the Beats 360 headphones, you might as well click out of this review now.

While a solid first attempt at this concept, I have some notes — so let's get into it.

Beats 360 at a glance

The pink/blue combo demanded a cute background. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The new flagship headphones from Beats run for $349.99 (the same intro price as the Beats Studio Pro) and come with the following features:

  • Battery life: 32 hours per charge

  • Microphones: Nine, with eight dedicated to ANC

  • IP rating: IPX4

The design of the Beats 360 can't be beat (so far)

The replaceable components meant allowed me to rock an all pink look. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The Beats cushion sets are the main draw of their design. The headphones themselves come in four different colors: black, cloud (cream), pink, and sky (light blue). You'll also have the option of purchasing an additional Performance Knit set in one of six different colors: black, cloud, pink, sky, navy, and volt (a neon green-yellow). The de-cushioned Beats surrounded by three cushioning options. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

It's plenty simple to swap out the cushions, which is why I was surprised by the case. These headphones encourage you to swap them out for different uses throughout the day, yet the magnetic closure case didn't consistently shut properly with an additional cushion set — sometimes it closed just fine, and sometimes I found myself needing to place the headphones and the components in the case just so if I didn't want it to hang open.

With the right positioning of the extra cushions, the case closed without issue. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable But sometimes, despite my best efforts, the case refused to close. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

Another oversight in the design I didn't love was the lack of a headphone jack and lack of USB-C listening functionality. It's not the end of the world, but it can limit listening options, especially while traveling or for anyone interested in lossless audio — a feature supported by the Beats Studio Pro.

The out-of-box sound on a pair of headphones could literally sound like thousands of angels singing in my ears, but if I'm paying $349.99, I would like the option to adjust my own sound signature. Maybe I want one less angel singing!

It's a concept Apple seems to understand with the recent iOS 27 update, which finally gave the AirPods Pro 3 a custom equalizer, but these Beats were left out of the software update.

At this point, the Beats 360 don't quite have the 360 appeal that they could with a few small changes. But middling ANC, OK sound, and zero equalizer for $350 makes it hard to recommend these wholeheartedly to the majority of folks, especially when cheaper headphones can manage all of the above, even if they don't feature the same customization. I anticipate that gym rats will love them — I look forward to the next iteration when the rest of us can, too.

Voice cloning scams: How to protect yourself from audio deepfakes

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your phone rings, and without thinking, you answer it. It's your spouse. Or your grandma. Maybe the friend you're always posting TikToks with online. And they're asking for your help. 

They've been in a car accident, and you have to provide some personal information as their emergency contact. They're in jail and need you to bail them out. Or maybe it's even simpler than that: Grandma can't log into her account and needs to send you a code. Can you please read it to her? 

The familiar lilt of their voices catch you off guard — only none of them are real. 

Their voices have been cloned, and malicious actors are donning their audio likenesses to scam you out of your personal information, and sometimes cold, hard cash. That number is growing, making even more people susceptible to the deceptive tech, but there are very simple ways to protect yourself and your loved ones.  

Scroll through TikTok, and you'll come across hundreds of stories detailing tales concocted by scammers to impersonate your loved ones. In one such video with more than 450,000 views, a young woman writes that her mother was nearly scammed by an imposter who imitated her grandmother's voice. The only way they caught it? Grandma is no longer alive. 

Scams are on the rise. In 2025, Americans lost around $148.2 billion to digital crimes, a jump of more than 25 percent from the year before. Recent data from digital security company NordVPN found that a growing number of scammers are pitching voice scam services to bad actors on dark web forums and Telegram channels. Sales pitches for voice cloning services rose sevenfold in the last two years, reported NordVPN.

Why more people are getting fooled by audio scams 

One reason that number keeps ticking up is the proliferation of sophisticated — but more importantly accessible — generative AI tools, explained Marijus Briedis, CTO at NordVPN. 

"Some years ago, a cloned voice had something slightly off about it, and most people could hear it. That is gone now," Briedis said. 

Advanced technology is only one part of the growing scam industry, however. Briedis explained that such tools are being increasingly packaged, priced, and sold directly to the Average Joe. 

Voice cloning tools, previously a standalone novelty tech, are now sold as a single feature in all-encompassing, common criminal toolkits, or "scam kits," experts told Mashable. These kits include access to legitimate generative AI tools via jailbroken accounts, troves of personal data, and other features sold at increasingly affordable prices. 

Credit: Cole Kan / Mashable / Getty

For example, a recent study of dark web marketplaces conducted by NordVPN found that a hacked ElevenLabs Scale account — complete with unlimited voice cloning — sold for just $150. An ElevenLabs API key was going for a mere $8.

Scam networks no longer need technologically savvy hackers or technologists. Just about anyone with a computer can do it. And that means you have to be prepared. 

Step 1: Know the threat and make a plan 

"When anyone can run the attack, volume becomes the strategy," Briedis said. "No single attempt has to be perfect. There just have to be a lot of them."

But asking someone to be on alert all the time is unfair, Briedis said. Up until now, the mantra has been: "Every message, every video, every call. Get it wrong once and that is the one that counts," Briedis explained. "Nobody holds that standard across a lifetime, and most security advice assumes they will."

As an alternative, users can prepare themselves, and their community, to be able to step up when the moment calls. 

Credit: Cole Kan/Mashable/Getty

Prepare your contacts

Eyal Elazar, head of market Intelligence for online merchant security business Riskified, described effective scam preparation as being a "digital ambassador" for your inner circle. 

It begins with a little research. Find and save verified contact information for important callers, such as your bank, insurance company, or family lawyers. You can use this to cross reference callers later.

Then, talk to your circles about what a legitimate phone call would look like. Know who lists you as an emergency contact, and in what contexts. Are you a beneficiary on an account, and, if so, what do you need to know?

Set a code word

Most experts Mashable spoke to recommend creating a code word that you can use with friends and family to confirm who they are. Don't share this anywhere online. "Sounds silly right up until the day it does not," Briedis said.

Filter out the red flags  

There are also common scam requests that are instant red flags, experts told Mashable. Any demand for money over the phone is suspicious. There are more specific tells, too: 

  • Requests to read a code, sent via text or email, over the phone

  • Requests for passwords or other log-in credentials 

  • Demands for payment via gift cards, wire transfers, crypto exchanges, and payment apps

  • Suggestions to initiate a wire transfer over a voice note, a phone call, or a voicemail

  • Demands of ransom

  • Law enforcement dealings conducted via phone call

Step 2: Putting down the phone can stop a scammer.

"AI isn’t inventing new scams, but it's removing the tells we’ve learned to look for in the old ones," said Donnie Hasseltine, chief information security officer at Arkstone Defense. 

It can be hard to discern the small audio tells that make your loved ones' voice truly theirs, especially when an individual is up against social engineering that forces urgency and stirs panic. 

So, the next piece of advice is to hang up on your friends or "friends." 

Yes, this may cause some drama in the group chat. But if you can't always tell when a voice is real or cloned, it's best to cut them off and reset. 

If someone you personally know was supposedly on the other line, call them back on the number you're used to before agreeing to anything. If it's a bank or your workplace, call a listed number on an official website, the back of a credit card, or a piece of mail. Many institutions are already keenly aware of common phone scams that target customers. 

"Stop judging the content and change the channel instead," Breidis urged. 

SEE ALSO: Have you been impacted by a scam or security breach?

Step 3: Slow down.

The last, and most simple, piece of advice is to take a breath. Experts told Mashable that urgency is the number one red flag that may indicate you are the target of a scam, e.g., someone claiming to be from the electricity company demanding money immediately in exchange for keeping your power on. That supposed urgency also sets you up to be more susceptible to scammers. 

Andrew Jones, co-founder of Adaptive Security, said that a manufactured sense of urgency as well as a demand for secrecy are the two factors found in nearly every successful scam he has studied. 

"Nothing is so urgent that it can’t wait for you to verify it," Hasseltine agreed. 

So don't give in to the rush. Slow down, hang up the phone, and ask someone else to weigh in. 

And it bears repeating: Never. Send. Cash.

Already been scammed? Here's what to do.

If a scammer has already gotten hold of your personal information or money, act fast:

  1. Block the scammer so they can't reach out again.

  2. Cut off their access to your bank account or payment method, if you provided that information. Call your bank immediately. If you sent a wire transfer or gift card, contact the issuer and tell the company you have been scammed. Change your passwords. Freeze your credit.

  3. Report your stolen information. Go to IdentityTheft.gov to create a government-recommended recovery plan. Report the scam to the Social Security Administration’s fraud hotline if you handed over your SSN.

  4. Stay calm. The vast majority of Americans have been scammed in one way or another. There is help out there, and you can prepare your defenses for next time.
