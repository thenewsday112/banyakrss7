Ready to read in color? These are the 4 best color e-readers.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When we write about e-readers, Kindle is usually the first name that comes to mind. But in the color e-reader market, Kindle has been late to the party. That delay opened the door for other brands to step in with stronger devices. We’ve rounded up the best color e-readers, and here are the ones that stood out.

Color e-reader price increases

This year has not been good for tech prices. Due to the global memory crisis, prices are increasing on tablets, phones, laptops, and even e-readers. Three out of the four color e-readers on this list increased in price this year. The only e-reader to avoid a price hike was the Kindle Scribe Colorsoft.

Temper your color expectations

An e-reader's color won't be quite as vibrant as a tablet's or physical book's. Credit: Samantha Mangino / Mangino

Having tested my way through a lot of e-readers — and now color e-readers — I’ve found a universal truth: While the colors are accurate, they often look a bit washed out. No matter which brand you use, that’s going to be the case. The muted tones don’t outweigh the convenience and portability of color e-readers, but it’s worth tempering your expectations.

Don’t expect the vivid, punchy colors of an iPad screen. An iPad has a max of 500 nits (a measure of brightness), while Kobo and Kindle e-readers max out at 100.

New to this guide:

  • As of October 2026, we added the new Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition to this list. While the display and resolution haven't improved, its new aluminum casing makes it a sturdier e-reader.

  • In February 2026, we added the Kindle Scribe Colorsoft to this guide. After testing Amazon's latest e-reader, we were thoroughly impressed by both its speed and display.

What about ReMarkable tablets?

You might notice that ReMarkable e-ink tablets are not on this list. We've tested both the ReMarkable Paper Pro and ReMarkable Paper Move, and while they're dynamic digital notebooks, they're limited in their e-reader capabilities. Getting e-books on these devices was a bit of a struggle, and we missed the Libby integrations available on Kindles and Kobos. So while we think they're great e-ink tablets, they're inferior e-readers.

DoorDash's CEO: Don't be 'delusional' after just a few hundred AI agent orders

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Some third-party AI agents have generated "several hundred" DoorDash orders, CEO Tony Xu said.

David Zalubowski/AP

  • DoorDash isn't seeing many orders through AI agents like Muse yet, its CEO said.
  • Third-party agents have generated "several hundred orders in total," Tony Xu said this week.
  • Even so, DoorDash is moving ahead with its own AI ordering tools.

The age of AI agents ordering your meals isn't here yet, according to DoorDash.

The food delivery service has received "several hundred orders in total" from agents such as Meta's Muse, DoorDash CEO Tony Xu said during the company's earnings call on Thursday. By comparison, DoorDash delivered 3.2 billion orders worldwide in 2025.

Instead, customers are still using DoorDash's app and website to place orders, Xu said.

"For the everyday ordering, what we see is people come to DoorDash, and they come back to DoorDash," he said.

Xu's comments suggest that buzzy AI agents like Muse and Instinct have far to go before they become a big part of how users buy things.

The agents can be useful for tracking the prices of holiday gifts or airline tickets, users told Business Insider earlier this month. Many are hesitant to let agents make the purchases themselves. They also use more secure payment options, such as single-use credit cards, in case the agents make a mistake.

Despite the low order volume through third-party agents, DoorDash is making changes to its own AI tools.

The company said in September that it was testing a text-to-order feature with an interface reminiscent of Instinct. Another ordering option, a chatbot called Ask DoorDash, creates an order from a recipe or shopping list and can adjust the contents to match customers' preferences.

Those AI tools could eventually generate new orders on DoorDash, Xu said.

"My take on all things technology is you never want to fight gravity," he said.

"But, at the same time, you do not want to be delusional," he added, referring to the low number of agent-placed orders.

The Beats 360 are eye-catching and impressively customizable, but does the listening experience match up?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you don't like the look of the Beats 360 headphones, you might as well click out of this review now.

That's not to say these headphones are only as good as their appearance — they do have their strengths — but if the many colorways Beats started teasing back in June via World Cup players wasn't enough of a hint, a big part of the allure of these Beats are how they look.

After spending a couple of weeks with these headphones, and switching out many an ear cushion and head band, I found myself more impressed with some aspects of these headphones, and surprisingly let down in others. While a solid first attempt at this concept, I have some notes — so let's get into it.

Beats 360 at a glance

The pink/blue combo demanded a cute background. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The new flagship headphones from Beats run for $349.99 (the same intro price as the Beats Studio Pro) and come with the following features:

  • Battery life: 32 hours per charge

  • Active noise cancellation: Yes

  • Microphones: Nine, with eight dedicated to ANC

  • IP rating: IPX4

  • Connection: Bluetooth 5.3, one-touch pairing for Apple and Google Fast Pair for Android users

  • Colors: Black, cloud, sky, pink

The design of the Beats 360 can't be beat (so far)

The replaceable components meant allowed me to rock an all pink look. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The Beats 360 aren't the first over-ear headphones to do swappable components: Dyson's OnTrac headphones launched with replaceable ear plates and cushions back in 2024. They did, however, also cost $499.99 and are less suited to using during exercise than the Beats.

The Beats cushion sets are the main draw of their design. The headphones themselves come in four different colors: black, cloud (cream), pink, and sky (light blue). Each headphone will come with its corresponding color in the Performance Knit cushion set, which closely resembles that found on the AirPods Max 2.

SEE ALSO: The 5 most comfortable headphones we've ever tested

You'll also have the option of purchasing an additional Performance Knit set in one of six different colors: black, cloud, pink, sky, navy, and volt (a neon green-yellow). Then there are two shades of the UltraPlush cushion set, which comes in black and cloud. The UltraPlush is the same faux leather material used on the Beats Studio Pro (and found on most over-ear headphones). That is to say, they're plenty comfortable for longer periods of wear and a nice alternative if you want to reserve the Performance Knit cushions for more high-activity use. All additional kits are priced at $49.99, but launch pricing has these marked down 80% for just $9.99.

The de-cushioned Beats surrounded by three cushioning options. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

It's plenty simple to swap out the cushions, which is why I was surprised by the case. These headphones encourage you to swap them out for different uses throughout the day, yet the magnetic closure case didn't consistently shut properly with an additional cushion set — sometimes it closed just fine, and sometimes I found myself needing to place the headphones and the components in the case just so if I didn't want it to hang open.

With the right positioning of the extra cushions, the case closed without issue. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable But sometimes, despite my best efforts, the case refused to close. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

Another oversight in the design I didn't love was the lack of a headphone jack and lack of USB-C listening functionality. It's not the end of the world, but it can limit listening options, especially while traveling or for anyone interested in lossless audio — a feature supported by the Beats Studio Pro.

Where the Beats 360 could definitely do better

The out-of-box sound on a pair of headphones could literally sound like thousands of angels singing in my ears, but if I'm paying $349.99, I would like the option to adjust my own sound signature. Maybe I want one less angel singing!

It's a concept Apple seems to understand with the recent iOS 27 update, which finally gave the AirPods Pro 3 a custom equalizer, but these Beats were left out of the software update.

Adjustable audio would've gone a long way for these customizable headphones. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

It's unfortunate, especially because the sound on these headphones not only didn't have the whole angel thing going on — it sounded kind of flat and bland, which feels decidedly un-Beats-like. The brand is known for its pumping bass, but here, the mids and vocals take center stage. Case in point: I was not bopping my head along to Lorde's "Supercut," which is a feat I frankly didn't know was possible. On a different point in the genre spectrum, Joni Mitchell's "Both Sides Now" sounded predictably beautiful because it's a Joni Mitchell song — the balance of sound, however, leaned too heavily on her vocals and shrank the guitar into the background. I found myself itching to adjust the equalizer settings while listening to the track.

The ANC isn't bad

Faring better than the sound is the ANC. Though it won't create the cocoon of silence some of Mashable's favorite noise-cancelling headphones are capable of, they'll definitely dim the sounds of a student-packed cafe playing loud punk music (trust me). That said, mid-range priced headphones are facing some serious competition from budget headphones these days, which are getting better and better at providing ANC that punches above its weight class.

The combo of the UltraPlush cushions and ANC made these headphones great for work. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

There's also the presence of some hissing that's somewhat noticeable without music playing — it's not nearly as loud as some cheaper pairs of headphones, but if you're sensitive to ANC that hisses, it's worth noting.

Are the Beats 360 worth it?

At this point, the Beats 360 don't quite have the 360 appeal that they could with a few small changes. The component customization makes it easy to envision a world where these are the headphones people take to the gym instead of AirPods Max, while still being a great pair of headphones to use for work, school, commuting — true all-arounders.

But middling ANC, OK sound, and zero equalizer for $350 makes it hard to recommend these wholeheartedly to the majority of folks, especially when cheaper headphones can manage all of the above, even if they don't feature the same customization. I anticipate that gym rats will love them — I look forward to the next iteration when the rest of us can, too.

High earners are feeling low

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Americans making six figures are feeling worried about the economy.

d3sign/Getty Images

  • Americans making more than $100,000 are feeling bad about their personal finances.
  • They're also feeling a lot worse about the government's economic policies.
  • Those personal and political concerns could impact Tuesday's midterm results.

Americans making six figures aren't feeling too hot.

Consumer sentiment is near historic lows, and according to Morning Consult's weekly tracking, households earning $100,000 or more have seen their sentiment drop to its lowest in months.

That dip is a worrying sign for spending — which is overwhelmingly powered by higher earners — and potentially pivotal as consumers gear up to vote in contentious midterm elections.

Late in the summer of 2025, 45.2% of six-figure Americans felt their finances were getting better. That dropped to 32.5% at the new year, and 30.2% in the week ending October 26.

Of course, lower-income workers still feel worse, especially as they're disproportionately impacted by soaring prices. Similarly, workers a bit further up on the income spectrum are also feeling strained, especially with inflation increasing more than wages in recent months. Middle-income workers surveyed by Pew Research said they don't have rainy-day funds that could cover three months worth of expenses, and just 47% said they were able to save money over the past year.

While higher-income households are still powering the consumer spending that's helping keep the economy afloat, that may be cooling. Year-over-year spending increases for the highest third of earners have slowed since June and are now similar to those of their middle-income counterparts, according to an analysis from the Bank of America Institute. The top 5% of earners, who earn far more than $100,000, still have the fastest spending growth, according to the BofA analysis.

At the same time, higher earners' perceptions of their own position have changed: Half of high-income families — who earn over $155,600 — identify as working class. High gas and oil prices have also begun to strain higher-earners, as inflation, powered primarily by soaring energy costs, remains high.

The declining sentiment comes as a U-shaped pattern emerges in economic perceptions: Both high and low earners are more likely than their middle-income counterparts to say they're living paycheck to paycheck. For higher earners, that lifestyle creep and even rising costs might be contributing to their vibes.

That continued dreariness among higher earners might prove particularly potent in the coming days. As swing state midterms loom, affordability is top of mind for many voters, and higher earners are no exception — they're increasingly disenchanted with the government's economic policy.

For politicians campaigning on affordability, that might mean a newly energized constituent base eager to bring down prices: Economically-worried six-figure voters.

Gen Xers don't believe they'll ever be able to retire comfortably

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Many Gen X workers say they may not be able to ever retire comfortably.

Willie B. Thomas/Getty Images

  • Gen Xers entering their 60s fear inadequate retirement savings and financial insecurity.
  • A Transamerica Center study found that the median Gen X worker has only $113,000 saved.
  • Job loss, caregiving, and age discrimination often challenge Gen Xers' retirement plans.

As the oldest Gen Xers enter their 60s, many fear they won't be able to live out the retirement of their dreams.

A Wednesday report from the Transamerica Center for Retirement Studies, determined that only 18% of Gen X workers were "very confident they will be able to fully retire with a comfortable lifestyle." One quarter strongly agree that they are currently building a large enough retirement nest egg to feel secure in retirement, a few percentage points lower than millennials and boomers.

Gen X's fears are grounded in reality. The median Gen X worker has $113,000 saved in total household retirement accounts, less than half of the $263,000 saved among baby boomer workers. It's a far cry from the $750,000 Gen Xers feel they need to have saved by retirement to feel financially secure.

Over the past year, Business Insider has spoken to dozens of Gen Xers navigating job searches, self-employment, and financial pressures from caregiving. Many older Gen Xers said that losing a job in their 50s derailed their careers. Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows that for those in the 55 to 64 age group, the average unemployment duration was over eight months in September, about seven weeks longer than those in the 45 to 54 group. Many of the Gen Xers BI spoke to blamed implicit age discrimination.

Some told Business Insider that they've taken substantial pay cuts, filed for bankruptcy, or arranged a break from work to develop AI skills. Many have had to draw from their 401(k)s or rely on loved ones to get by after draining their savings. A few said they grew tired of the job hunt and turned to starting a business instead.

Many Gen Xers, born between 1965 and 1980, have been pushing back their retirements. More than one in three in the Transamerica survey said that they would likely not retire until 70 or older, or wouldn't retire at all. More than half anticipated they would still work in retirement to some degree.

The survey suggests Gen X on the whole feels less secure about its retirement than their older and younger peers. Less than a quarter of Gen X expects they could retire before 65, half the rate of Gen Z.

Retiring early — even if temporarily — is a common trend among caregivers caring for loved ones. The survey found that more than a third of workers are currently or were previously caregivers. Among them, 35% said their financial situation has worsened while providing care. As the survey notes, millennials and Gen Xers are slightly more likely to bear the brunt of the work compared to other generations.

Family caregivers are spending over $11,500 in out-of-pocket expenses, and though many have taken on second jobs, others have put their careers on hold in their late 40s and 50s to assist their parents or spouses. A few gave up six-figure salaries and rely on savings, public pensions, or a spouse's disability insurance to get by.

"When a whole host of people leave the workforce, or when people take three or four years off, their family suffers, and society suffers" from the economic hit, said Samir Shah, CEO of care navigation platform CareScout.

Some of this angst can be traced to having started saving later than their younger colleagues. The average Gen Xer started saving through a 401(k) at age 30, compared to age 26 for millennials. Many Gen Xers began working when 401(k)s were first introduced into workforces after the decline of defined-benefit pension plans. Upwards of eight in 10 Gen X workers are offered a 401(k) or similar plan, while nearly half believe those instruments will be their primary retirement income source.

Another 27% of Gen X workers anticipate drawing primarily from Social Security to fund their retirement, though three-fourths of respondents express some concern that Social Security would be insolvent by the time they retire.

Despite somewhat more promising retirement-related statistics, younger and older generations also expressed financial woes in the survey. Among Gen Z workers, six in 10 feel burned out, with half struggling to make ends meet. Many cited paying off debt, building emergency savings, and covering basic living expenses as their current financial priorities, amassing a median savings amount of about $37,000.

Nearly six in 10 millennials cited debt as a deterrent to their retirement savings; 37% serve as caregivers, nearly all of whom made changes to their working arrangements.

For boomers, over half either don't plan on retiring or expect to after 70, and just over a quarter have a written retirement strategy. Preparing for long-term care and outliving their savings are boomers' greatest retirement fears.

Voice cloning scams: How to protect yourself from audio deepfakes

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your phone rings, and without thinking, you answer it. It's your spouse. Or your grandma. Maybe the friend you're always posting TikToks with online. And they're asking for your help. 

They've been in a car accident, and you have to provide some personal information as their emergency contact. They're in jail and need you to bail them out. Or maybe it's even simpler than that: Grandma can't log into her account and needs to send you a code. Can you please read it to her? 

The familiar lilt of their voices catch you off guard — only none of them are real. 

Their voices have been cloned, and malicious actors are donning their audio likenesses to scam you out of your personal information, and sometimes cold, hard cash. That number is growing, making even more people susceptible to the deceptive tech, but there are very simple ways to protect yourself and your loved ones.  

Scroll through TikTok, and you'll come across hundreds of stories detailing tales concocted by scammers to impersonate your loved ones. In one such video with more than 450,000 views, a young woman writes that her mother was nearly scammed by an imposter who imitated her grandmother's voice. The only way they caught it? Grandma is no longer alive. 

Scams are on the rise. In 2025, Americans lost around $148.2 billion to digital crimes, a jump of more than 25 percent from the year before. Recent data from digital security company NordVPN found that a growing number of scammers are pitching voice scam services to bad actors on dark web forums and Telegram channels. Sales pitches for voice cloning services rose sevenfold in the last two years, reported NordVPN.

Why more people are getting fooled by audio scams 

One reason that number keeps ticking up is the proliferation of sophisticated — but more importantly accessible — generative AI tools, explained Marijus Briedis, CTO at NordVPN. 

"Some years ago, a cloned voice had something slightly off about it, and most people could hear it. That is gone now," Briedis said. 

Advanced technology is only one part of the growing scam industry, however. Briedis explained that such tools are being increasingly packaged, priced, and sold directly to the Average Joe. 

Voice cloning tools, previously a standalone novelty tech, are now sold as a single feature in all-encompassing, common criminal toolkits, or "scam kits," experts told Mashable. These kits include access to legitimate generative AI tools via jailbroken accounts, troves of personal data, and other features sold at increasingly affordable prices. 

Credit: Cole Kan / Mashable / Getty

For example, a recent study of dark web marketplaces conducted by NordVPN found that a hacked ElevenLabs Scale account — complete with unlimited voice cloning — sold for just $150. An ElevenLabs API key was going for a mere $8.

Scam networks no longer need technologically savvy hackers or technologists. Just about anyone with a computer can do it. And that means you have to be prepared. 

Step 1: Know the threat and make a plan 

"When anyone can run the attack, volume becomes the strategy," Briedis said. "No single attempt has to be perfect. There just have to be a lot of them."

But asking someone to be on alert all the time is unfair, Briedis said. Up until now, the mantra has been: "Every message, every video, every call. Get it wrong once and that is the one that counts," Briedis explained. "Nobody holds that standard across a lifetime, and most security advice assumes they will."

As an alternative, users can prepare themselves, and their community, to be able to step up when the moment calls. 

Credit: Cole Kan/Mashable/Getty

Prepare your contacts

Eyal Elazar, head of market Intelligence for online merchant security business Riskified, described effective scam preparation as being a "digital ambassador" for your inner circle. 

It begins with a little research. Find and save verified contact information for important callers, such as your bank, insurance company, or family lawyers. You can use this to cross reference callers later.

Then, talk to your circles about what a legitimate phone call would look like. Know who lists you as an emergency contact, and in what contexts. Are you a beneficiary on an account, and, if so, what do you need to know?

Set a code word

Most experts Mashable spoke to recommend creating a code word that you can use with friends and family to confirm who they are. Don't share this anywhere online. "Sounds silly right up until the day it does not," Briedis said.

Filter out the red flags  

There are also common scam requests that are instant red flags, experts told Mashable. Any demand for money over the phone is suspicious. There are more specific tells, too: 

  • Requests to read a code, sent via text or email, over the phone

  • Requests for passwords or other log-in credentials 

  • Demands for payment via gift cards, wire transfers, crypto exchanges, and payment apps

  • Suggestions to initiate a wire transfer over a voice note, a phone call, or a voicemail

  • Demands of ransom

  • Law enforcement dealings conducted via phone call

Step 2: Putting down the phone can stop a scammer.

"AI isn’t inventing new scams, but it's removing the tells we’ve learned to look for in the old ones," said Donnie Hasseltine, chief information security officer at Arkstone Defense. 

It can be hard to discern the small audio tells that make your loved ones' voice truly theirs, especially when an individual is up against social engineering that forces urgency and stirs panic. 

So, the next piece of advice is to hang up on your friends or "friends." 

Yes, this may cause some drama in the group chat. But if you can't always tell when a voice is real or cloned, it's best to cut them off and reset. 

If someone you personally know was supposedly on the other line, call them back on the number you're used to before agreeing to anything. If it's a bank or your workplace, call a listed number on an official website, the back of a credit card, or a piece of mail. Many institutions are already keenly aware of common phone scams that target customers. 

"Stop judging the content and change the channel instead," Breidis urged.

Step 3: Slow down.

The last, and most simple, piece of advice is to take a breath. Experts told Mashable that urgency is the number one red flag that may indicate you are the target of a scam, e.g., someone claiming to be from the electricity company demanding money immediately in exchange for keeping your power on. That supposed urgency also sets you up to be more susceptible to scammers. 

Andrew Jones, co-founder of Adaptive Security, said that a manufactured sense of urgency as well as a demand for secrecy are the two factors found in nearly every successful scam he has studied. 

"Nothing is so urgent that it can’t wait for you to verify it," Hasseltine agreed. 

So don't give in to the rush. Slow down, hang up the phone, and ask someone else to weigh in. 

And it bears repeating: Never. Send. Cash.

Already been scammed? Here's what to do.

If a scammer has already gotten hold of your personal information or money, act fast:

  1. Block the scammer so they can't reach out again.

  2. Cut off their access to your bank account or payment method, if you provided that information. Call your bank immediately. If you sent a wire transfer or gift card, contact the issuer and tell the company you have been scammed. Change your passwords. Freeze your credit.

  3. Report your stolen information. Go to IdentityTheft.gov to create a government-recommended recovery plan. Report the scam to the Social Security Administration’s fraud hotline if you handed over your SSN.

  4. Stay calm. The vast majority of Americans have been scammed in one way or another. There is help out there, and you can prepare your defenses for next time.

Copperfield! The New Musical review: A rollicking, big-hearted Dickens adaptation that is not to be missed

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Originally published as a serial, starting in 1849, Charles Dickens' The Personal History, Adventures, Experience and Observation of David Copperfield the Younger of Blunderstone Rookery is a tale rife with tragedy. But recent adaptations like Armando Iannucci's 2019 movie The Personal History of David Copperfield and Jeffrey Scharf's Copperfield! The New Musical focus on the hope and happiness found in a life story rocked repeatedly by woe.

However, for those taking notes on such things, Copperfield! does hew more closely to the events of Dickens' story, embracing some of its darkest moments without reserve. And yet, this new musical, now playing Off-Broadway, is a rapturous delight — perhaps in part because it doesn't shy away from its hero's hardships.

Copperfield! A New Musical is a classic story told with a raucous and sensationally talented ensemble.

Amelia Cormack, Jed Feder, Alexander Sovronsky, Henry Witcher, Cathryn Wake, and Nancy O'Connor in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

With book, music, and lyrics by Scharf, Copperfield! is set across England, from 1835 to 1870. Directed and choreographed by Gabriel Barre (with associate director and choreographer Ashley Wren Collins), this musical kicks off with a grown David Copperfield (Bobby Conte) reflecting on his life. A dashing man, he ushers audiences back to the day of his birth, introducing his widowed mother Clara (Cathryn Wake) in fervent labor, while her devoted maid Peggotty (Amelia Cormack) assists, and his eccentric aunt Betsey Trotwood (Kara Mikula) flutters about making bizarre predictions and demands.

With an energetic song called "Betsey Trotwood Copperfield/It's a Boy," Mikula kicks the story off with rousing energy and plenty of spunk. Then comes Young David (Calvin Knegten at the performance I saw), who shoulders much of the first act, with his older counterpart stepping in on occasion to make observations or lend reflective moral support. Young David will be bounced on his mother's knee, then he's off to a seaside village to visit with Peggotty's charming family, before being sent to a cruel boarding school at the behest of his vicious stepfather, Mr. Murdstone (Will Reynolds).

From there, the poor boy will be orphaned then forced into hard labor, before seeking salvation from the aforementioned aunt. Even as he finds relief from one vexation, other troubles arise. But so, too, do meaningful relationships with debonair classmate Steerforth (Owen Squire Smith), scrappy debtor Mr. Micawber (Andy Taylor), a befuddled but brilliant family friend Mr. Dick (Aaron Michael Ray), and his benevolent bestie Agnes Wickfield (Tatiana Wechsler).

Now, as the story roams from the Copperfield estate to Yarmouth, London, and beyond across decades, Copperfield! keeps its cast almost alarmingly small. Around Conte's strong central performance, 13 adult actors not only play several characters each, but also pick up instruments to join in the orchestration. In this way, they turn a tiny stage — enhanced by flanking platforms and a modest but efficient set design — into a wide world that is cruel, kind, and often wondrous.

Bobby Conte leads a marvelous ensemble cast in Copperfield!

Bobby Conte and Cathryn Wake in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

As an admirer of Iannucci's adaptation of David Copperfield, I thought of it often while witnessing Copperfield! In that, I found a curious distinction. Iannucci focused on David's pursuit of identity, drawing attention to how he was forced to code-switch and even change his name depending on his company. Scharf's approach is less cerebral, focusing instead on the search for home.

The plays opening number "I Am Home" is reprised throughout the show, as David reflects on when he felt safe and thought his happy ending was assured. Of course, whether you know Dickens' work or just that this musical is unlikely to end in the first act, this reprise toys with our hearts, because we know there's more to come. While the company brings out guitars, trumpets, pianos, drums, and more instrumentation, Scharf's songs range from dastardly ("Mr. Murdstone Is the Master Now") to playful ("I Shake Your Hand Sir") to mournful ("Always the Pleasure Was Mine") to rollicking ("I Am a Rover"). In this, Scharf creates a thrilling odyssey in a simple setting, using his music and relying on this sensational cast to build David's world with their unbridled vigor.

While the whole of the cast sings their hearts out, there are a few standouts. Zohran Mamdani's 'revolution' isn't a socialist dream or capitalist nightmare

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostafa Bassim/Getty Images; BI

Zohran Mamdani became mayor of New York City on a simple, focused economic platform: fast and free buses, universal childcare, cheaper groceries, and a rent freeze. His supporters envisioned him ushering in a fresh economic order. His critics feared the worst — a cabal of heavy-handed leftists who would plunge the city into fiscal chaos.

A year after Mamdani's election, New York's economy doesn't look like the stuff of socialist dreams — or capitalist nightmares. Zohranomics is unfolding at a piecemeal pace. Unable to unilaterally raise taxes on the rich, the mayor is championing a pied-à-terre tax on wealthy residents who don't live in the city full-time (or say so on their tax filings). His municipal grocery store endeavor is a modest five-location work in progress.

The city is focused on the "fast" half of the bus plan and has put the "free" part on the back burner. Like his predecessors, Mamdani is dedicating a lot of time and resources to housing. He orchestrated a rent freeze on 1 million rent-stabilized apartments — one of his most controversial and impactful moves so far, though not unique to his administration. Many of the mayor's policy moves have been administrative measures: cutting red tape for small businesses, rooting out government inefficiencies, pension maneuvering to balance the city budget. And, the administration is trumpeting small-scale wins on filling potholes and making it easier to cancel subscriptions.

The list of accomplishments sounds more like a standard Democratic think-tank wish-list than a Bolshevik manifesto. Mamdani is governing like a leftist populist with the instincts of a city operations nerd — but his biggest economic bets are yet to be made.

New York is an incredibly rich city, with a private wealth pool of over $3 trillion and an annual GDP of roughly $1.3 trillion. It's also a city with stark economic divides. It has a 25% poverty rate, meaning more than 2 million New Yorkers live below the poverty line. (In comparison, the national poverty rate is 10%.) From 2019 to 2024, almost two-thirds of New York City's income growth went to the top 1%, according to the city's comptroller, compared to 39% nationally. The costs of childcare, housing, and basic necessities are increasingly daunting. Those economic dynamics propelled Mamdani to the mayorship.

The list of accomplishments sounds more like a standard Democratic think-tank wish-list than a Bolshevik manifesto.

"A lot of people love this city, but not as many of them can live here," the mayor said in a recent podcast interview with comedian Theo Von.

But the immutable truth facing any New York City mayor is that there is not much they can do to influence the economy. Much of New York's financial engine runs on its own. New York's state government sets most of the city's taxes. The national and global economies affect New Yorkers' pocketbooks, too.

Mamdani's program is to, where possible, wield the city's influence to lower costs and shift some of New York's power structures toward the traditionally less powerful. That means pairing rent regulations with initiatives to stimulate more homebuilding and pressuring the state to secure funding for a new free 2-K program. The city is weighing public options where the private markets fail to deliver, such as the grocery store initiative and new government-backed property and liability insurance for landlords. It's investing $30 million into an NYU quantum computing lab in exchange for a cut of its profits. And, it's pushing the idea that the wealthy could stand to chip in more and pay higher taxes.

Mamdani's programs are universal instead of means tested. Many middle- and upper-middle-class will be able to put their kids in free 2-K programs and live in rent-stabilized apartments.

Michael M. Santiago/Getty Images

This is a more expansive vision than a standard anti-poverty agenda — and a politically riskier one. New York is a crowded, raucous city where almost everyone sees themselves as an underdog, from billionaires to bodega owners to beleaguered renters. And when everyone feels like the system is stacked against them, any change is bound to make someone feel newly wronged.

To his progressive allies, Mamdani's approach is an attempt to prove that local government can directly respond to constituents. The hope is that tackling and touting the little things, like snow shoveling and trash pickup, will build trust that they can use to address the big things. It doesn't sound as radical as socialism, says Elizabeth Wilkins, the president and CEO of the Roosevelt Institute, a progressive think tank, but it's "radical in the degree to which people have lost faith in the idea that the government cares about how they experience the economy or that the government could do anything about it."

Housing is the arena where the mayor has made the biggest difference to New Yorkers on both material and rhetorical fronts. In June, the mayor-appointed Rent Guidelines Board voted to enact a two-year rent freeze on about 1 million rent-stabilized apartments in New York City, affecting an estimated 2 million residents. Meanwhile, he has advanced the argument that the root of the city's affordability crisis is a lack of housing and taken big steps to construct more. He's set out to build 200,000 more affordable homes and preserve another 200,000 over the next decade. His administration has identified city-owned land that could be turned into housing. They've created a task force to speed up affordable housing production and eliminate administrative barriers and delays, from tweaking the city's housing lottery system to reforming environmental review processes. The mayor has even nudged President Donald Trump to allow building on public sites, such as Sunnyside Yards, a rail hub in Queens.

"Each change may be modest, but they are important and collectively can add up to some meaningful changes," says Ingrid Gould Ellen, a professor of urban policy and planning at NYU, of the administrative efforts.

Early in his tenure, Mamdani launched "rental ripoff" hearings, providing a platform for tenants to air their frustrations. It signaled to the city's renters that he's on their side and cast bad landlord behavior as both a housing-quality problem and a consumer-protection problem. Mamdani has extended an olive branch to landlords in announcing an initiative to lower their insurance costs, but the shift in priorities is making many of them feel unfairly targeted.

"The tone put us on edge," says Ann Korchak, the board president of the Small Property Owners of New York.

Rent freezes aren't a new idea — they were put in place three times under former Mayor Bill de Blasio on a similar number of rent-stabilized apartments. The policy's proponents point to that as evidence that the sky won't fall this time around. Its critics note that the freezes haven't curbed the upward march of the city's housing costs in the past and argue that this time would be especially detrimental, since landlords' costs are higher and increasing faster than they were a decade ago.

Then there's the question of how the funding will come together for Mamdani's ambitious housing plans, and how much private developers and lenders will play ball. The rent freeze, which guarantees at least two years of flat revenues for rent-stabilized buildings, could lead to higher borrowing costs and financing challenges for builders, says Howard Slatkin, the executive director of the Citizens Housing and Planning Council. Broader economic conditions that shape financing — most notably interest rates — are entirely out of the administration's control. Building and operating housing is expensive, and "we haven't yet seen what they are thinking will move the needle on those costs," Slatkin says. Building code reform, for example, could make the math work better. The administration is looking at it, but it's a slog.

Not all public land is suitable for housing, as parcels may be awkward shapes and sizes or have zoning and legal limitations. The administration has released a tracker that identifies over 100 city-owned sites it says could yield more than 50,000 homes. Some of this space isn't empty: In Bensonhurst, the city is seeking to redevelop the existing New Utrecht Library as a brand-new library plus 200 apartments.

The pied-à-terre tax is a financial knock to those subject to it, but it's a minuscule part of New York's overall fiscal picture. The mayor's office estimates the tax could bring in $500 million annually, though the comptroller says it could be $340 million-$380 million, given exemptions and the likelihood some homeowners would change their behavior to avoid the tax. For context, the city's most recent annual tax revenue was $85.4 billion.

Success depends on how many units are built, and their size, location, and cost. It also matters how fast construction moves, since delays are expensive. By the city's own estimate, New York needs 700,000 new homes over the next 10 years to address its housing shortage, meaning 70,000 homes a year, well above the net increase of 43,800 new units the city had in 2025. The Mamdani administration itself has acknowledged that it can take eight years for an affordable housing project to be conceived to a family actually being able to move in. It's seeking to cut eight months from the process and shrink the timeline from endeavors requiring zoning changes by two years.

"To make a serious dent in our housing crisis, we need to build more quickly and efficiently," Gould Ellen, the professor, says.

Mamdani has recast the deputy mayor for economic development role as deputy mayor for economic justice, a move meant to signal an administration focused on fairness, not only growth. He put in place a tenacious regulator at the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, which has tried to convey that the city is willing to use its weight to address relatively mundane headaches by cracking down on bad actors in food delivery, self-storage, and even dentistry. Emily Eisner, executive director of the Fiscal Policy Institute, a progressive think tank, says she sees much of the DCWP's actions as "small potatoes" in terms of actual affordability, but there's something "symbolically important" about people seeing the government stand up for them.

On small business, the administration has released 50 proposed reforms meant to cut red tape. They've also put forth ballot proposals to speed up processes for building ramps and sidewalk cafes in public spaces and reduce paperwork for city contractors. On big business, as much as Mamdani's rhetoric and political affiliation as a democratic socialist spooked some leaders, his mayoralty hasn't caused a corporate exodus. Anthropic, Fanatics, and Deloitte decided to expand in Manhattan this year. His bet is that corporations will stay and thrive in New York without City Hall bending over backward for them.

The best economic policy may be for the Knicks to win the NBA championship every year, jokes Andrew Rigie, executive director of the NYC Hospitality Alliance.

"People oftentimes, even more than getting what they want, they want to feel seen and heard," Bradley Tusk, a venture capitalist, political strategist, and Andrew Cuomo ally, says. For CEOs and big business, that's not happening as it did with other administrations, Tusk says. A City Hall spokesperson points out that the mayor has put together a Business Advisory Council, hosted an international business roundtable during the United Nations General Assembly, and has held multiple meetings with prominent business leaders, including JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon and Goldman Sachs' David Solomon.

New Yorkers may enjoy small financial reprieves, but the bigger challenge is bringing down the cost of bigger-ticket items. Mamdani's plan to open five city-run grocery stores is unlikely to move grocery prices significantly in a city of over 1,000 grocery stores and 10,000 bodegas and corner stores. The administration says it's chosen densely populated neighborhoods for the grocery store sites in hope of making a large direct and indirect impact on New Yorkers in those areas.

Julie Samuels, the president and CEO of trade group Tech: NYC, says that Mamdani has moderated in City Hall, though he hasn't yet focused as much on job growth and economic development as administrations past. One place he has succeeded: the vibes.

"Economic policy is also dictated by whether or not their employees want to be somewhere," she says. "And if employees want to be in New York, then the tech companies will be here, then the banks will be here."

Reflected in the nature of Mamdani's policy proposals is a belief that the city's livability and affordability problems aren't reserved for the poor. In turn, his programs are universal instead of means tested, so that any New Yorker can access them, regardless of income. Many middle- and upper-middle-class will be able to put their kids in free 2-K programs and live in rent-stabilized apartments that now won't get more expensive for a couple of years. If the buses ever do become free, that will apply to everyone who rides them.

Many of Mamdani's biggest promises can't be fulfilled in Manhattan but instead up in Albany, where the state legislature and Gov. Kathy Hochul have the reins. Any plans to raise taxes or increase the minimum wage depend on a statehouse three hours away.

James Parrott, a labor economist at The New School and the city's chief economist for two years under former Mayor David Dinkins, says the city has "never had a mayor who has made such a concerted drive to get Albany to do more to help." Mamdani has campaigned aggressively for Hochul, deploying his armament of clever viral videos to help get her reelected.

Closer to home, a major collision is brewing with city labor. Large public-sector union contracts are coming up for renegotiation, including District Council 37 and the United Federation of Teachers, which each have tens of thousands of members. The city itself is New York's largest employer. Soon, the mayor will find himself seated across the negotiating table from the labor he pledged to champion on the campaign trail. Because New York City generally relies on pattern bargaining — where one major union's agreement sets the standard for others — the stakes are high for all of the city's employees. They're also high for a mayor who wants to signal that he values the city's municipal workforce while keeping the city's finances on a solid footing.

Mamdani will, like every mayor, have to make a number of hard choices, including ones that don't grab headlines. He sued the New York City Council to block a bill that would grant a one-time $10,000 bonus to public school paraprofessionals because it falls outside collective bargaining. He was able to close the city's budget gap by restructuring the city's municipal pension payments, but accounting maneuvers can't replace structural shortfalls. If tax revenues flatten or state aid from Albany stalls, the administration will have to choose between scaling back on core goals or making painful spending cuts. This isn't the kind of stuff that goes in a press release or a TikTok video.

"The administration has been really, really focused on getting short-term, press-friendly wins," says Michael Kinnucan, health policy director at the Fiscal Policy Institute and a longtime progressive activist. "It sends a message about where its concerns are."

I saw a finance career as a route to FIRE

When I was deciding on a career, I asked myself what I could study that would get me to financial freedom the fastest.

In the final five years of my career, I earned $150,000 a year on average. I saved roughly 45% of my earnings. Because I wanted to retire early, I was aggressive with my portfolio allocations to achieve faster growth.

I'd always envisioned being a fully present parent, but I was working nine to 12 hours a day. The thing that would give me the most peace of mind before our daughter arrived was to stop working entirely.

I handed in my notice in January, a month before we moved to Ecuador. It was bittersweet. Part of me enjoys the corporate environment.

We could have afforded to retire early and stay in the US, but the bigger question was what we wanted our lives to look like. Cost of living was part of the equation. We came to Ecuador on a whim in November 2025, at my wife's suggestion, and fell in love with it immediately. We knew it was the place for us.

Cuenca is a big city with a small-town vibe. It doesn't take long to get anywhere. By the end of our first week visiting, we'd already made quite a few friends at coffee shops.

Our rent is $1,120 less than our mortgage payments

We moved in February. It was a stressful process. We had a whole home's worth of things to sell and donate.

During our trip in November, we visited hospitals, looked at furniture and appliance prices, viewed apartments, and explored the city. We connected with a realtor and locked in our place before we arrived.

On the day we landed, our place was ready to go. Our rent is about $600 a month, compared with a monthly mortgage payment of $1,720 in the US. We sold our home recently because I didn't want to be a landlord in a different country.

Healthcare is cheaper in Ecuador

Our daughter was born in April, three weeks early, and my wife had a prolonged hospital stay after a C-Section. The birth cost us $2,790. In the US, it likely would have cost us several thousand more.

Previously, we were paying about $300 each a month for health insurance in the US. In Ecuador, we pay as we go because insurance and out-of-pocket costs are similar. On average, we spend about $50 a month taking the baby to the doctor.

One of the most challenging things about the move is that we're thousands of miles away from our family. As first-time parents, we find it tough that our parents can't pop in for a weekend to help. We miss some Western luxuries, like getting an Amazon package in a couple of hours, and our Spanish isn't perfect, but when I look at my daughter, I don't miss anything.

I value the freedom and the time I get with my daughter. There are so many side projects I can explore.

My only regret is not doing this sooner.

How Pures 24-hour chat timer forces daters to skip the endless banter

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

One of the major annoyances of online dating is how often matches devolve into pen pals rather than translate into IRL dates. Those initial, flirty conversations are great, don't get us wrong, but most of us aren't jumping onto Tinder or Hinge just to endlessly text. And yet, in modern dating, where both people often feel afraid to make the first move and take the match into the real world, endless texting is all too often the only result of a once-promising match. 

But it doesn't have to be this way. Dating app Pure concocted some clever solutions to the never-ending chatting, and in our own experimentation, they work like a charm. 

Here's how Pure introduced a sense of urgency into its dating mechanics, and how that helps users translate matches into real-life encounters. 

Opens in a new window Credit: Pure Pure   Learn More

How Pure leverages a sense of urgency to benefit its users

One unique feature of Pure is that it doesn't use traditional dating profiles, which means its matches operate differently than on basically every other dating app. 

Instead of having a permanent, public-facing profile, Pure users can post "ads," temporary personal advertisements shouting out who they are or what they want, whether that's for a long-term relationship or a short-term connection. It's only when someone has posted an ad that they're discoverable to other users, and only then are your basic profile elements (age, gender, sexual orientation, and photos) visible. Once your ad expires, so does access to your profile.

The result of this mechanic is a sense of urgency, the very real possibility that you're seeing someone you may never see again, so if you want a connection, you have to act fast. That's a very welcome change from the dating app norm, where profiles are always visible, and you can browse them at your convenience, whether on your morning commute or between sets at the gym.

But Pure takes things a step further with how you actually interact with other people. 

The disappearing chat mechanic

From the moment you initiate a chat on Pure, you're under an artificial time constraint, since every chat automatically self-deletes after 24 hours, unless both parties agree to suspend the chat timer. 

While at first this seems like an unnecessary constraint, in practice it's actually very healthy and helps daters avoid the endless banter that plagues so many online dating interactions. 

Think about what would happen if you approached a cute stranger in the real world. You may see them at a bar or coffee shop, going about their day, but no matter how smooth your introduction, you know in the back of your mind that you can't monopolize this person's time and attention for very long. 

For an in-person approach, you have to be concise in your communication: I think you're cute and want a chance to get to know you better — would that interest you? But on an app, where there's no sense of urgency, you can endlessly waffle, delaying the most important question of all in favor of a never-ending series of mundane questions. 

This is why Pure's disappearing chat mechanic is so clever, even when both parties agree to disable it, since even that act forces a mutual acknowledgment of the connection. And after using the app over an extended period, we can confirm that Pure's culture really encourages in-person interactions at the expense of endless chatting or swiping.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button is now playing at the Public Theater until Dec. 20.
