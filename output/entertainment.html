The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

SEE ALSO: 'Copperfield! The New Musical' review: A rollicking, big-hearted Dickens adaptation that is not to be missed

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

SEE ALSO: 'Memoirs of Amorous Gentlemen' review: Duncan Sheik's musical adaptation gets kink right, but falters elsewhere

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button is now playing at the Public Theater until Dec. 20.

The singer was performing the first of two ‘Harryween’ concerts at Madison Square Garden last night (October 30), marking the penultimate date of his mammoth 30-night residency at the venue.

For the occasion, Styles initially appeared dressed as Elvis Presley, wearing the King’s signature white jumpsuit, complete with a cape and gold sunglasses. His backing band and dancers also embraced the Halloween theme, dressing as various versions of Styles from throughout his career, including looks inspired by his appearances at Coachella, the Grammys and the Met Gala.

Later in the show, however, Styles changed into an altogether different costume, recreating the clothes he wore for his original audition on The X Factor in 2010.

He went on to perform ‘Isn’t She Lovely’, the Stevie Wonder classic that helped launch his career when he sang it for judges Simon Cowell, Nicole Scherzinger and Louis Walsh at the age of 16. Styles also jokingly recreated one of his memorable comments from the audition, telling the Madison Square Garden audience: “I work in a bakery.”

The original audition saw Styles arrive as a teenager from Holmes Chapel, Cheshire, where he had been working part-time at a bakery.

The costume changes were part of Styles’ revived ‘Harryween’ tradition, which first saw him hold two special Halloween concerts at Madison Square Garden in 2021.

On the opening night that year, he dressed as Dorothy from The Wizard Of Oz and performed ‘Somewhere Over The Rainbow’, with his band dressed as other characters from the 1939 film.

The following evening, he wore an elaborate clown costume and covered Britney Spears‘ ‘Toxic’.

The tour comes in support of his fourth solo album ‘Kiss All The Time. Disco, Occasionally’, which was released in March and received a four-star review from NME.

Reviewing the ‘Together, Together’ tour in Amsterdam in May, NME awarded the performance five stars, praising its sense of community and describing it as “a hopeful celebration of community and connection”.

Styles has also announced further tour dates for 2027, including additional Wembley Stadium concerts and support from Kylie Minogue, LCD Soundsystem and more.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 will remove real-world footage of the 2022 Seoul crush incident after severe backlash in South Korea. Bereaved families issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of these videos, and Activision has since vowed to cut them from the title.

The game is set in the Korean Peninsula, and real-world footage of several events is shown in the opening cutscene as a means to ground its narrative in reality.

Among the videos are scenes of the 2022 Seoul crush incident, which happened during a Halloween celebration on Oct. 29 in Itaewon, when a crowd suddenly surged and began to trample or crush those within it. Around 160 deaths were confirmed, most of whom were young adults, making it one of the deadliest events in South Korean history.

Families of those who perished or were injured in the incident issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of this footage, telling the BBC they were “appalled.”

“We cannot help but be appalled that the chaotic and desperate scene, in which many citizens at the site performed CPR to save one another and victims lay on the freezing street, was used to create a scene in a game,” they said.

“Using such an event as material for a game made for entertainment is in itself highly inappropriate and senseless,” they added.

Modern Warfare 4 is largely set in the Korean Peninsula during a “New Korean War.” Image via Activision

Read what’s behind the latest news in gaming (with a bit of snark) by subscribing to the Destructoid Checkpoint newsletter!

Activision has since responded to the backlash, promising to cut the scenes entirely in the game’s next update on Nov. 12. The company told the BBC it never “intended to reference or trivialize the Itaewon disaster or any other real-world tragedy in any way, shape or form,” but merely used real-world footage to frame the game’s fictional story.

The Modern Warfare franchise, both the original trilogy and these new games, is well known for its shocking and mortifying scenes. However, the difference here is that, while the most horrific moments in the games were all fictional, Infinity Ward used real footage of a tragedy. We can debate whether showing it here was distasteful or dehumanizing or reductive, but I can see where these families are coming from.

If we start drawing lines in the sand too much, though, art will have no way to push forward, and addressing reality will become impossible. Someone somewhere is going to be inevitably offended by your story, your characters, and what you’re saying, or rather, what they’re saying about the state of the world, but I think that is ultimately a fair price to pay.

My Chemical Romance have released a new animated short film, ‘Prelude To The End’, featuring voice contributions from Billie Eilish, Phoebe Bridgers and others – watch below.

  • READ MORE: My Chemical Romance – ‘The Black Parade’ review

The film was made in collaboration with visual artist Lauren Tsai and arrives as the band continue celebrating the 20th anniversary of their landmark 2006 album ‘The Black Parade’.

Produced by Tsai’s independent studio UNREAL INC., the project combines the visual world of her animated film The Dying World with ‘The End.’, the opening track from the album.

The nine-minute film follows Astrid, a student at a mysterious New England boarding school that has become overrun with rubbish.

When the school’s authorities throw away her cherished sketchbook, she follows it down a rubbish chute and finds herself transported into a surreal world inhabited by forgotten ideas.

Tsai voices Astrid herself, while Eilish plays her friend and Bridgers voices her sister. Japanese video game designer Hideo Kojima plays a character known as The Teacher, while Scrubs actor John C. McGinley voices The Dean.

The project marks a full-circle moment for Tsai, who grew up creating animated fan videos set to My Chemical Romance’s music.

“It was an absolute dream to work on this project for The Black Parade and The Dying World,” Tsai said in a statement.

She added that she deeply respected frontman Gerard Way‘s creative legacy, and that she was honoured he had drawn inspiration from her own work, having spent years admiring the worlds he created.

Tsai previously worked with Bridgers in 2023, when she directed the animated music video for Boygenius’ ‘Cool About It’.

Speaking to NME about that project, she said: “‘Cool About It’ is a beautifully melancholic and piercing song that puts words to feelings often unexplained. It is an absolute dream to collaborate with the genius that is Boygenius.”

Bridgers is also a longtime admirer of My Chemical Romance, having covered ‘Welcome To The Black Parade’ alongside Sloppy Jane at SXSW in 2022.

Meanwhile, Eilish and Bridgers have previously performed together, with the latter joining her alongside Boygenius bandmates Lucy Dacus and Julien Baker for a rendition of ‘When The Party’s Over’ at London’s Electric Ballroom in 2023.

‘Prelude To The End’ will also be screened ahead of My Chemical Romance’s final two Hollywood Bowl concerts tonight (October 30) and tomorrow (October 31).

The shows will conclude the US leg of their ‘Long Live The Black Parade’ anniversary tour, which has seen the band perform the 2006 record in full alongside selections from the rest of their catalogue.

The performances have made headlines after the company behind the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders launched legal action against the band over the use of lookalike uniforms during a staged gunfight.

Meanwhile, MCR released an expanded 20th anniversary edition of ‘The Black Parade’ last Friday (October 23), featuring six bonus tracks, including previously unreleased recordings from a 2007 show in Hoboken, New Jersey.

In its original 9/10 review, NME praised the record’s theatrical ambition, writing that it belonged alongside ‘American Idiot’, ‘Doolittle’ and ‘Nevermind’ among the greatest American rock albums.

Elsewhere, Way recently told fans that the group hope to record another album, although no plans have been confirmed.

“We’ve kind of really always wanted to make another record, so we hope that life allows us to do that,” he said. “You guys deserve another record. I don’t know when it’ll be. But it’ll be awesome.”

Star Wars Battlefront 3 has just been added to the list of reasons why BioWare and Mass Effect are no longer with us, a new report says.

BioWare’s doom had been spelled out over the past decade after multiple failed releases (Mass Effect Andromeda, Anthem, and Dragon Age: The Veilguard), but according to Bloomberg’s Jason Schreier, it was the development price tag that really sealed the deal…and a galaxy far, far away was involved in the decision-making process, too.

Screenshot by Destructoid

“Financial pressures” mounting as part of the $55 billion purchase of EA led by Saudi Arabia’s investment fund that left the publisher $18 billion in debt meant that cuts were coming, and some cuts are easier to make than others, especially considering when it comes to two similar spacefaring sci-fi franchises: Star Wars and Mass Effect.

With both games set to cost “hundreds of millions of dollars,” EA and its executives chose the iconic film franchise over BioWare’s RPG series (this really kind of feels like a no-brainer in hindsight, unfortunately for me and many other Mass Effect fans), and decided to also shut down the erstwhile studio as a part of its cost-cutting measures.

“Star Wars Battlefront 3 has not even begun development yet, but EA executives saw it as a less risky, more predictable bet than a new Mass Effect — and the publisher behind Battlefield has always been more comfortable with shooters than RPGs,” Schreier said.

There’s no debating that many factors went into BioWare’s closure, but the biggest has to be the fact that the studio hasn’t made a game reflective of its past successes in quite some time. It starts and ends there. But I do find it interesting and a little bit sad that Star Wars was also a factor in Mass Effect’s demise, given that they are two of the most beloved sci-fi franchises ever made.

This feels especially tough to swallow considering that EA decided to go with a live-service FPS over a singleplayer narrative-driven RPG, because that’s the least surprising part of all of this.

The legendary actor, perhaps best known for his work on Star Trek as Captain Picard, has passed away, according to a statement posted to his Instagram, which was first reported by The Hollywood Reporter. The statement also disclosed that theYorkshire-born actor had been living with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.

Born in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, in 1940, Stewart joined the Royal Shakespeare Company at the age of 26 before becoming a household name as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation from 1987 to 1994. He later revisited the role in four feature films and the three-season Star Trek: Picard series. He also played Professor Charles Xavier in X-Men. He was knighted in 2010, and published his memoir, Making It So, in 2023.

“We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles,” the statement reads, noting that he was “in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny” after “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia.” It added that “He will be remembered for the dignity, compassion and humanity he brought to every role,” and asked for donations to Alzheimer’s research and caregiver charities in lieu of flowers.

You don’t pick up a Suzanne Collins novel expecting a happy ending, but Sunrise on the Reaping completely and utterly shattered me in a way no other book has before. I went in braced for a brutal tragedy—it’s a Hunger Games prequel, after all—but what I wasn’t prepared for was how profoundly it would move me.

Sunrise on the Reaping is, without a doubt, the most beautifully tragic book I’ve ever read. When I finished reading it for the first time back in March 2025, I spent about a full hour sobbing. I wish I were exaggerating, but that’s how much this story hurt, and how deeply it moved me. I honestly don’t think I’ll ever recover.

Image by Kacee Fay via Destructoid

I’ve been into The Hunger Games series since the first book debuted in 2008, and I knew Haymitch had to have a tragic backstory, so I thought I was at least a little bit prepared for what would unfold in this novel. Boy, was I wrong, though. No amount of preparation could have softened the blow of this story—and yet, it’s this very devastation that makes it one of the best books I’ve ever read.

Sunrise on the Reaping tells Haymitch Abernathy’s story, set 24 years before the events of the first The Hunger Games book. Even if you’re only lightly familiar with this series, you probably know Haymitch—he’s one of the most unforgettable characters. When we first meet him in the later series, he’s drunk, abrasive, sarcastic, and full of bitterness. But as the series progresses, we glimpse the broken man beneath the bravado. We always knew he’d been through hell, we just didn’t know the full extent—until now.

Haymitch’s story begins with instant tragedy, as on his 16th birthday, he avoids being called in the Reaping, only to be forced into the Games anyway after one of the chosen tributes tries to escape and is killed. In the aftermath, his girlfriend, Lenore Dove, steps in to comfort the victim’s grieving mother. When Haymitch intervenes to protect Lenore from the Peacekeepers, he’s illegally reaped in the place of the fallen tribute.

Image via Lionsgate

The version of the games Haymitch is playing is the Quarter Quell, which means there are twice as many tributes sentenced to death in the arena, making for 48 children sentenced to this fate, and only one who makes it out alive. We know that survivor is Haymitch, but knowing the outcome doesn’t soften the blow. The deaths of the other 47 tributes still land with devastating weight.

My favorite novel from this series before reading this one was Catching Fire, and Sunrise on the Reaping is a spiritual predecessor to it in every way. Without giving too much away, you learn far more about characters from the original trilogy than you might expect, and you’ll never see those books or films the same way again. Everything Haymitch does and says has a whole new meaning after reading this, and he’s not the only one.

Like the tributes in Catching Fire, Haymitch finds himself at the heart of a plot to sabotage the arena. This was a twist I didn’t see coming, and one I knew wouldn’t end well, seeing as the games went on for many years after. Still, I couldn’t help but hope things would somehow turn out differently than they were fated to. Even knowing how it ends, I found myself rooting for Haymitch and the others every step of the way.

Image via Lionsgate

The beauty of this novel is that, yeah, you know it’s gotta end badly and that Haymitch is going to experience some truly traumatizing stuff, but you have no idea how it all unfolds until you’re deep in it. You know Haymitch is going to end up a belligerent drunkard, despite his repeated insistence that he doesn’t drink throughout this book, but you’ll never predict what gets him there.

Like every book in this series, Sunrise on the Reaping delivers powerful, timely messages and political commentary woven seamlessly throughout the narrative. It’s also got some surprisingly fun literary references, like the recurring use of the poem “The Raven” by Edgar Allen Poe, which is hauntingly tied to both Haymitch and his lover, Lenore Dove.

Just when you think you’ve witnessed the worst this story has to offer, it hits you with another gut punch. This cycle continues right up to the final pages, where you’re met with perhaps the most devastating blow of all. If you ever think you’re having a bad day, remember: Haymitch Abernathy is having a far, far worse one.

Image via Lionsgate

If you’ve only ever read the main trilogy, I can’t recommend delving back into the larger world of The Hunger Games enough. Start with The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, which provides chilling depth to President Snow’s backstory, then enjoy Sunrise on the Reaping, a devastating companion that ties it all together in ways you won’t see coming.

I was so skeptical of expanding the world of Panem beyond the main books, but both of these reads are every bit as powerful as the original trilogy, and maybe even a bit more haunting. Haymitch’s fate was sealed long before this book began, yet Sunrise on the Reaping still makes you love, hurt, and grieve— holding on to hope that things might turn out differently, even when you know the story was always meant to break you.

Skateboarding legend Tony Hawk has once again teased the possibility of a new game in the Tony Hawk Pro Skater series. While celebrating the 25th anniversary of the original title on social media he suggested that “there will be a future” for the series. It isn’t the first time Hawk has made this kind of comment, either, after saying, earlier this month, that he had been in talks with Activision again and went on to say “we’re working on something.”

The original Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater launched in 1999, first on the PS1 and then on the N64 soon after. The title was a massive hit and still has a critical score of 92, and a user rating of 8.2 out of 10 according to Metacritic.

The success of the original spawned several sequels with most proving popular with gamers. Pro Skater 5 on PS4, which was the last new entry in the series, was something of a blip, getting roundly panned by reviewers and gamers alike. However, a remaster of the original two games saw the games return to successful ways.

Since the remake, fans have been waiting for news of another launch with some calling for a remake of the third game. Others are hoping for something meatier with a possible Pro Skater 6 launch.

Hawk previously stated, in 2022, that he and Activision were looking to find somebody to remake the third and fourth games but they didn’t trust anybody the way they trusted Vicarious.
