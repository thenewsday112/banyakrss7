NASAs Voyager 1 gives mission control another moment of panic

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA's farthest spacecraft gave mission control a scare when it inexplicably went silent and missed two days of planned check-ins. 

The nail-biting incident happened just two weeks ago, when the Deep Space Network didn't receive a call from Voyager 1 on either Oct. 18 or 19. But before the agency could send repair commands, the spacecraft, now outside the solar system, signaled back on Oct. 20. No communication outages have happened since. 

Engineers still don't know what caused the problem. So far, the team has discovered that one set of thrusters shut down, leaving a backup set to take over. The thrusters are small engines that point and steady the spacecraft. Voyager 1's computers do this automatically whenever they detect an issue with the active thrusters to ensure the spacecraft is in a safe mode. 

"This is the first time we've seen the spacecraft do something like this, but we are also at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected," said Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, in a statement. "We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened."

Voyager 1's trouble adds to a growing list of signs that NASA's longest-running mission is winding down. Its plutonium power source loses about four watts every year, forcing engineers to turn off two science instruments to conserve power. On Nov. 18, the spacecraft is expected to reach a historic milestone of one "light-day" from Earth. That distance, roughly 16.09 billion miles away, measures how far light travels over 24 hours.

Voyager 1 has traveled farther than any other human-made object and is racing through space at 38,000 mph.

Launched in 1977 to survey the outer planets, the spacecraft and its twin Voyager 2 have explored four planets — Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune, and Uranus — 48 moons, and a host of planetary magnetic fields and rings.

In August 2012, Voyager 1 was the first to enter interstellar space. The two remain the only spacecraft ever to leave the solar system, though New Horizons is trying to make its escape as well.  

Both Voyagers run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which are power units that turn heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. In September, NASA engineers stretched their shrinking power supplies by turning off three instruments on each and replacing them with three lower-powered alternatives — a plan they nicknamed "the Big Bang." The devices involved in the swap also help keep the spacecraft warm enough to work in the freezing cold of space, so the swaps had to happen at the same moment to spread heat evenly.

That strategy should keep their Cosmic Ray Subsystems, Magnetometers, and Plasma Wave Subsystems running for at least one more year, according to NASA. The devices measure high‑energy particles, the local magnetic field, and ripples in charged gas around the spacecraft.

Once Voyager 1 passes one light day, a radio message from Earth will take 24 hours to reach it, and its reply will take another 24 hours to come back.

"If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response," said Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Turning a solar power system into its own backup

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Rather than directly converting photons into electricity, concentrated solar energy uses mirrors to turn sunlight into heat, which can then be used to drive traditional generating turbines. Up to about a decade ago, there was a case to be made that concentrated solar energy had a bright future, as improvements in heat storage had turned it into a way to generate power around the clock.

It wasn't to be. While concentrated solar costs fell enough to compete with nuclear power, the price of photovoltaics plunged, making solar panels the cheapest source of power in most locations. Currently, if you have a good site to generate solar power, then the economics of PV panels are hard to beat.

That has left us needing to build a mixture of other facilities—typically batteries and natural gas facilities—to handle overnight needs or weather-driven shortfalls. But a small company is promising that it can help avoid the need to build a separate facility. Its version of concentrated solar is designed so that a fossil-fueled backup system shares most of the generating hardware with the solar side.

Scientists think theyve just discovered a new dwarf planet like Pluto

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have just spotted a small, bright world in the outer reaches of the solar system they think could be a dwarf planet like Pluto. 

The mysterious space object, for now known as 2026 ED138, lies about 46 times farther from the sun than Earth, and may measure between 370 to 930 miles wide. Scientists estimate it's about 1.1 billion miles farther than Pluto in the Kuiper Belt, a zone of icy debris past Neptune.

Matthew Holman, a researcher at the Harvard & Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, made the discovery two weeks ago by running data from the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory through a program he built with fellow astronomer Kevin Napier. 

"My first reaction was one of excited disbelief," Holman said in a statement. "Objects like this are very rare, so I immediately started checking whether I or the code had done something wrong."

The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center, the official recordkeeper for new objects in our solar system, announced it as a candidate on Wednesday. If confirmed, ED138 could become the sixth officially recognized dwarf planet in the solar system, following Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. 

SEE ALSO: Life ingredients probably didn't ride in on a comet, new study shows

Rubin's detection of a previously unknown world in our own cosmic backyard highlights a new accelerated way of doing astronomy. After the initial find, the team was able to piece together a detailed "picture" of the object in three days. By pairing the observatory's repeated sky scans with archival data and pictures from other telescopes, the researchers learned quite a bit about ED138 and its stretched orbit around the sun. 

You can think of a dwarf planet as a scaled-down planet. It gets pulled around the sun by its own gravity, but it's not quite massive enough to clear other space rocks out of its way like a regular planet does. Pluto's 2006 demotion from planet status occurred because other icy bodies cross its orbit. 

"Once we identify a new object, we can search back months, and sometimes even decades, finding it in images where nobody knew to look," said Mario Jurić, Rubin's lead solar system scientist at the University of Washington, in a statement. "This ability to both discover and 'precover' objects is transforming how quickly and accurately we can determine their orbits and understand the distant solar system."

Astronomers say ED138 shines so brightly that folks with fairly small telescopes could probably see it. The reason no one has noticed it until now may be that it hides in plain sight among the Milky Way's crowded band of stars in the sky.

Looking back in the vault, Rubin's Simonyi Survey Telescope had observed ED138 some 30 times between March 4 and July 11. Then, using the Asteroid Institute's ADAM Precovery tool, which combs archives for old matches to newly found objects, the team saw it again in 2016 images taken with the Dark Energy Camera at another observatory in Chile. Combined, the data allowed astronomers to trace the object's path over a decade. 

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory took these images of 2026 ED138, a possible dwarf planet beyond Pluto that may be one of the largest and brightest objects ever found that far from the sun. Credit: NSF–DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory / NOIRLab / SLAC / AURA / M. Jurić / D. de Martin / M. Zamani

For now, scientists call ED138 a dwarf planet candidate because they still need to prove it's round. Icy objects of this size usually get pulled into a round shape by their own gravity. To be sure, though, scientists need to observe it with other telescopes in infrared light — a kind of light our eyes can't see — to pin down its exact size and other details.

Distant objects like ED138 are artifacts from the era when the planets, including Earth, formed, and they could help explain how the solar system and its giant planets came together.

Over the next 10 years, Rubin will photograph the whole southern sky repeatedly. Scientists believe this new detection is just the beginning of many discoveries to come. 

"Any others hiding in the places earlier surveys skipped are now within reach," Holman said.

A hidden ocean change near Antarctica could have global consequences

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The rapid decline of Antarctic sea ice since 2016 has triggered a dramatic shift in a crucial part of Earth’s climate system, scientists say.

In a paper published Wednesday, researchers documented a sharp salinity increase in a vast layer of the Southern Ocean, extending from 2,300 to 4,000 feet deep and encircling Antarctica. The shift reverses decades of freshening and echoes ocean changes usually associated with major global climate shifts over millennia but is now happening in just a few years.

The switch from a decades-long trend of freshening water to increasing salinity has implications that could extend beyond Antarctica. The layer, known as Antarctic Intermediate Water, spreads northward into the Indian, Pacific, and Atlantic oceans, transporting heat, carbon, oxygen, and nutrients toward the tropics, and even across the equator, said co-author Bishakhdatta Gayen, a researcher and professor at the University of Melbourne and at the Centre for Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences in Bengaluru, India.

Life ingredients probably didnt ride in on a comet, new study shows

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When comets hit Earth billions of years ago, they pelted the planet too fast and too steep for them to have delivered fragile organic molecules, according to a new study. 

Using advances in computer simulations, a University of Cambridge-led team of researchers ran scenarios of comet strikes, comet interactions with an atmosphere, and gravity's influence on comet movements through space to determine whether they could have brought the chemical ingredients for life to Earth. 

Rather than look at a bunch of important molecules, the researchers focused on one in particular: hydrogen cyanide, or HCN. Made of one hydrogen, one carbon, and one nitrogen atom, it's a simple molecule commonly carried on comets. They found that the immense heat would have literally vaporized most of those molecules on impact, challenging a theory on the origins of life that has existed for about a century.

Even in the rare case that HCN survived a crash to Earth, it would not have come in large enough quantities, said Richard Anslow, lead author of the paper, which appears in the journal Scientific Reports.

"It would really have to be a one-in-a-million occurrence," said Anslow, a Cambridge astronomer, in a statement. "We can't rule out for certain that it happened that way. But it seems to be a really rare occurrence — at best, a one-off fluke." 

SEE ALSO: Webb spotted a subtle difference in the interstellar comet's ice

Comets are chunks of ice, dust, and rock left over from when the solar system began about 4.6 billion years ago. Most range from a few hundred yards to dozens of miles wide, and they travel on long, stretched-out orbits. When a comet swings close to the sun, its heat converts some of the comet's ice into gas, which creates the glowing cloud and long tail. Scientists long suspected they helped start life on Earth.

In 1992, scientists Christopher Chyba and Carl Sagan studied the moon's craters and found that space dust, not comets, brought most of the carbon that reached Earth. But space dust holds its carbon in tough compounds that don't easily react, so it couldn't supply something like HCN. 

The Rosetta space mission took images of comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko in November 2014. Credit: ESA / Rosetta / NAVCAM

Because comets do carry HCN in a usable form, they have remained a contender as a source of life's building blocks. Lab experiments show that concentrated HCN can produce fats, sugars, and the nucleic acids that make up DNA and RNA.

The researchers selected HCN as the best case study because it's the only starter molecule that comets carry in useful amounts, and its triple bond makes it tougher than more complex molecules. In short, the scientists figured if HCN can't survive a comet crash, others chemicals probably won't either.

Recent computer models show that only comets wider than about 300 feet make it through the early Earth's atmosphere without breaking apart or burning up. Those models also show that earlier studies overestimated how much HCN can actually survive a high-speed collision. 

But if not comets, what could have brought life's ingredients? The researchers suggest asteroids, which slammed into Earth far more often. Their impacts may have triggered the critical chemical reactions to make prebiotic molecules. Or, an even simpler source: Maybe Earth made them on its own, without any extra help from space. 

The study showed comet delivery was slightly more probable for Mars. It's smaller than Earth with a thinner atmosphere, so comets had a better chance of reaching its surface intact. But the more likely answer is that it didn't happen on either planet, Anslow said. 

"Historically, scientists thought that comets were important for the origin of life on Earth because they carried carbon and nitrogen-bearing molecules, so these giant space snowballs were almost like a get-out-of-jail-free card that brought all the molecules a planet would need to seed life," Anslow said. "It’s kind of a romantic idea, but our results suggest it's highly unlikely."

Sculpture re-creates odd optical effects of famed Lycurgus Cup

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Dating back to around 400 CE and made of dichroic glass, the Lycurgus Cup is notable for exhibiting different colors depending on the light. (It gets its name from the scene depicted on its surface, of King Lycurgus of Thrace.) Light it from the front, and the cup looks green; light it from behind, and the color changes to a deep red. University of Sheffield scientists have re-created the original recipe to produce a contemporary glass sculpture with the same optical effect.

The cup is considered one of the seven glass wonders of the world, one of just 40 glass cage cups that survived. Exactly why the unusual color effects of the Lycurgus Cup occurred was a mystery until 2007, when a team of scientists discovered the color shifts were due to the presence of nanoparticles in the glass—the result of Roman artisans adding finely ground gold and silver into the mix. Those nanoparticles change how the electrons in the cup vibrate in response to light so that the color one sees shifts with the observer’s vantage point.

As we reported in 2019, it’s a similar phenomenon to the one responsible for the shifting colors of 19th-century daguerreotypes due to the scattering properties of nanoparticles. The Romans added salt when they melted the silicon (sand) to make the glass, and those salts contained gold atoms. In the melting process, those atoms aggregate and produce nanoparticles, which in turn produce the colors. It’s how any stained glass is made. It's also similar to how the precise lattice-like structure of photonic crystals produces iridescent colors in nature, like the wings of butterflies, or opals.
