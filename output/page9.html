Anthropic takes steps to block Claude from reverse-engineering video games

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

According to reporting from Tom's Hardware, Anthropic's Claude AI has quietly started restricting the kinds of work it will do, including modding, decompiling, or reverse-engineering video game code. And though there has as yet been no official announcement about why, the reason is still pretty obvious.  

The video game community has been in the news a lot lately, first because of the GTA6 leaks that went mega-viral and spurred Take-Two Interactive into getting subpoenas against both Microsoft and Discord in the hopes of finding the responsible parties, and next because the hacking and modding community discovered how much easier AI makes their job.

SEE ALSO: Claude's new smart reports tell bosses when workers are 'off-topic'

First, game decompilers went into overdrive, playing around with the source code of multiple different video games to, for example, put Nintendo's Mario inside the dark gothic world of Elden Ring, or import Spider-Man into the Batman: Arkham City game, seamlessly blending the mechanics of one character into the world of another. Next came the ports: World of Warcraft, Call of Duty, and Grand Theft Auto: Vice City made playable in a browser, without the original developers or copyright owners having any say in the matter. 

These initial modifications and ports were already giving gaming companies nightmares, teasing a future without system gatekeeping, but things really got out of hand with the PlayStation 5 jailbreak, which resulted in everything from backward compatibility with older-gen PlayStation games to cross-platform play, with users figuring out how to run Xbox-exclusive titles like Halo 2 and acclaimed Nintendo titles like Zelda: Breath of the Wild on a jailbroken PS5.

Given the potential number of lawsuits these jailbreaks could cause, and the astronomically high nature of the monetary damages, it's no surprise that AI companies are scrambling to shut down or severely restrict these pathways, but it's also deeply ironic. As Tom's Hardware reporter Bruno Ferreira notes, this sudden concern for copyright "highlights the irony of frontier AI companies that copied the world's information and art for their training sets and are now concerned with copyright infringement." 

There's also the ugly truth that, if one company makes a gesture toward respecting copyright law or basic ethics, the bad actors can find another LLM that won't (or hasn't yet) taken the same precautions, creating a kind of AI arms race driven by perverse incentives: the least ethical AI might be poised to become the most popular.

Vintage photos show what London looked like 100 years ago

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Flappers at the bar of a nightclub in London, circa 1925.

General Photographic Agency/Getty Images

  • A century ago, London was a bustling city, filled with culture and beautiful architecture.
  • Poverty, however, remained an issue, with slum dwellings in parts of the city.
  • Take a look at what life was like in London in the 1920s.

Today, London is a bustling, metropolitan city, and the same could be said about the city 100 years ago. 

Like today, London was known for its attractions such as Piccadilly Circus, Buckingham Palace, and the River Thames.

But many photos show parts of the city that are unrecognizable now.

From the glamorous fashion all the way to the slum dwellings, take a look at these photos from 1920s London to see how much life has changed.

An aerial shot of Trafalgar Square in London shows that parts of the city are still structurally the same 100 years later.

DE AGOSTINI PICTURE LIBRARY/Getty Images

In the early 1800s, the square was meant to be a cultural center of the city, and it officially got its name in 1830 after several transformations, according to the Greater London Authority.

At the time, Trafalgar Square was the perfect place for children to frolic in fountains.

Mirrorpix/Getty Images

The fountains were built in 1845.

Oxford Street no longer has any cars or buses.

Fox Photos/Getty Images

While Oxford Street remains lined with stores and restaurants, it has recently been pedestrianized. As of October, vehicles are no longer permitted to enter between Orchard Street and Great Portland Street.

Piccadilly Circus had a similar scene of hustle and bustle 100 years ago.

Topical Press Agency/Getty Images

If you look closely, you can see the double-decker buses that are still popular today. 

Piccadilly Circus has always been a popular destination in London.

Universal History Archive/Getty Images

Piccadilly Circus was created in 1819, and by 1920, the area had become a popular crossroads. Today, it has become a tourist destination and a must-see when visiting London.

There were even traffic jams on the bridges that stretched across the River Thames back in 1920.

Fox Photos/Getty Images

Today, there are 35 bridges across the River Thames.

The pedestrian bridges also got crowded.

Fox Photos/Getty Images

This image shows men in 1920 crossing a bridge over the River Thames on a windy day. 

For those who wanted to avoid the road traffic, the London Underground was always an option.

Topical Press Agency/Getty Images

The London Underground — also known as the Tube —  was the first subway system in the world, opening in 1863.

Despite its reputation among locals these days for being uncomfortably warm, 100 years ago, the London Underground was advertised as "cooler below" during the summer.

Bicycles were also a popular way to get around London.

Topical Press Agency/Getty Images

These bikes belonged to employees at Somerset House, which overlooks the Thames.

The 1920s also saw the growth of civil aviation as a result of the light-aircraft movement. London Flying Club Flight instructor F R Matthews, Stag Lane Aerodrome, Edgware, 1928.

Topical Press Agency/Hulton Archive/Getty Images

In August 1924, the British Air Ministry announced the establishment of 10 flying clubs in response to the development of the light airplane for the Lympne trials.

In December of that year, the Royal Aero Club was selected to form the first club in the London District. 

By 1928, there were 21 clubs in operation, more than 3,000 members, and more than 12,000 hours of flight time logged during the year, according to General Aviation News.

At the time, the River Thames had a largely underdeveloped waterfront.

cjp/Getty Images

Today, most people know the waterfront of the River Thames to be a lively place. Most notably, there is the London Eye, which is a 443-foot Ferris wheel, but it wasn't built until 1999.

The Houses of Parliament did, however, stand on the Thames at the time.

Science & Society Picture Library/Getty Images

The construction of the Houses of Parliament began in 1837.

The party leaders certainly looked different 100 years ago, though.

Central Press/Getty Images

Here, the politicians showcased clothing for upper-class men, which included suits, hats, and walking sticks.

Of course, Buckingham Palace was standing a century ago and looked as it does today.

Print Collector/Getty Images

Buckingham Palace was completed in 1705 and has undergone some renovations since. Today, the palace is the official headquarters for the royal family.

But the royal family inside the palace looked very different.

DEA / ICAS94/Getty Images

At the time, King George V was sitting on the throne of England. He was King Charles' great-grandfather.

Likewise, 10 Downing Street hasn't changed much over the last 100 years.

Mirrorpix/Getty Images

Since 1735, 10 Downing Street has acted as the home and office for the British prime minister.

But at the time, Stanley Baldwin was calling the famous residence home. Stanley Baldwin outside 10 Downing Street in 1926.

Fox Photos/Hulton Archive/Getty Images

Stanley Baldwin was the leader of the Conservative Party and was in office three times: between 1923 and 1924, 1924 and 1929, and 1935 and 1937.

In 100 years, women's clothing has changed dramatically — back then, the flapper became a symbol of the '20s.

George Rinhart/Getty Images

During World War I, women went to work in place of men and experienced newfound freedoms. In response, a new woman emerged: the flapper. These women cut their hair short, wore loose clothing, and were regarded as reckless, according to Historic UK.

Both men's and women's fashion saw a trend toward casual simplicity. Fashionable women arrive at Lord's cricket ground for the Eton vs. Harrow match in 1928.

Fox Photos/Getty Images

The trends of 1920s fashion saw women in shorter, simpler dresses and loose-fitting garments, while men wore more casual suits, according to the Museum of London.

Suffragists fought for women to achieve the same voting rights as men. A group of suffragists picket outside the House of Commons, February 29, 1924.

Bettmann/Getty Images

It wasn't until 1928 that women over 21 were finally given the same voting rights as men, increasing the number of women eligible to vote to 15 million, according to the UK Parliament. 

The emergence of the automatic telephone was both a technological advancement and an erasure of the first female workforce in Britain. Visitors putting calls through as they learn how to use the automatic telephone during a class at the Science Museum in South Kensington, London, England, 2nd September 1929.

Keystone/Hulton Archive/Getty Images

The late 19th and early 20th centuries led to a booming workforce of "hello girls," or female switchboard operators who connected calls at central telephone exchanges. 

However, the introduction of automatic telephone exchanges allowed callers to dial numbers, as shown above, leading to the disappearance of one of the first female workforces in the UK, according to the Science Museum.

For leisure, people would sometimes go to bathhouses and lidos.

Topical Press Agency/Getty Images

In the UK, a lido refers to a public outdoor pool. While there was a boom in lidos in London in the 1930s, some had already been built in the 1910s and 1920s, such as the Chiswick Baths and the Tooting Bec Lido, according to the London Museum.

The Chiswick Baths, which opened in 1910, were seen as a place for "promoting freedom, frolicking, and fun," according to the British Film Institute.

Dance halls were purposeful entertainment centers for people throughout London. The dance floor at the Palais de Dance, Hammersmith, London; March 1, 1920.

Topical Press Agency/Hulton Archive/Getty Images

The 1920s are notable for the emergence of dance halls, or "palais de danse," with the first being the Hammersmith Palais, according to research by James Nott, a historian of twentieth-century British history.

Most people also loved playing amateur football.

Popperfoto/Getty Images

Football has long been a popular sport in England.

Slums continued to exist in the city in the 1920s. Boys boxing barefoot in a street at Shadwell, London.

Firmin/Getty Images

Before the government began slum clearance in the 1930s, certain parts of London were characterized by slum housing, with people living in densely packed, impoverished conditions.

A new Science study on Manx shearwaters, seabirds that can fly hundreds of kilometers a day over the North Atlantic, shows they jet-lag themselves during their excursions. And because these birds navigate by the Sun, that jet lag leaves a measurable mark—it skews their compass.

Clockwork compass

Patrick Lewin is a behavioral and sensory ecologist at the University of Oxford and lead author of the study. “Manx shearwaters are medium-sized birds, about the same size as a pigeon,” Lewin said. “They are white underneath and black on top, but they have very long wings, and they are closely related to albatross.” Manx shearwaters nest in burrows on coastal islands around the North Atlantic. Most breed around the UK and Ireland, with about half of their population living in Wales.
