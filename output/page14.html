Bright Eyes’ Conor Oberst opens up about being “really close to the edge” with personal struggles and finding it “almost impossible” to write about Trump era

Conor Oberst has opened up about being “really close to the edge” when he faced personal struggles, while saying it’s “almost impossible” to write music focusing on the era of Donald Trump.

During an appearance on the New York Times‘ Popcast, the Bright Eyes frontman and soloist was asked about the “near-death experience” he’d spoken of in an interview with the LA Times earlier this year.

  • READ MORE: Bright Eyes on new album ‘Five Dice, All Threes’, and why “Elon Musk is one of the biggest pieces of shit”

“I didn’t see the light or anything like that, but it was very real,” he responded in the new conversation, but did not share any specifics about what had happened (via Stereogum). “I didn’t really feel happy or sad or anything like that. I actually had the meanest internal voice, being like, ‘Haven’t you wanted this your whole life, you fucking son of a bitch?'”

Oberst continued: “When I was really close to the edge, there was nothing good about it. I could keep doing what I’m doing and probably be dead really fast, or I could change up my style and try to stay around and make more things and don’t be in such a hurry. Life’s so short…”

He went on to say that “people need me around”, adding: “My family need me around, my friends. It’s one of those things that I think people find different reasons to live for and all that sort of thing.”

Oberst’s previous frank discussion with the LA Times saw him address concerns for his wellbeing after Bright Eyes cancelled some live shows in 2024 due to the singer losing his voice. However, fans speculated at the time that Oberst was experiencing other issues.

“I was not in good shape to sing,” he told the publication, saying that his problems at the time were “private business”. Oberst said he was “doing so much better” after making “some lifestyle changes”.

“I’m not gonna say I’m sober because I’m not,” he shared. “But I gave up the worst of it. I’m trying to go on with life and not dwell on it. But any kind of near-death experience, it does completely change your outlook on the world.”

He also told the LA Times that there was “a temptation to cancel everything the next year too”, referring to Bright Eyes’ 2025 UK and European tour and their shows in North America. “I begged everyone: ‘I have to go on tour – I need motivation’,” he recalled.

“I mean, I have a psychiatrist. She’s actually really helped me. The struggle is never-ending – being human is hard. I’m feeling gratitude for the fact that I have a huge support system, which is probably why I’m still here.”

Elsewhere in the new Popcast chat, Oberst was asked how his attitude towards folk and protest songs differed under President Trump from being under the last Republican POTUS, George W. Bush.

“It’s really hard for me to even articulate the Trump era,” he replied. “Maybe at some point I’ll be able to do it. You can’t satirise or whatever what is already like a parody, and a disgusting, gross cartoon, you know?”

He went on to mention Bright Eyes’ song about Bush, 2005’s ‘When The President Talks To God’, explaining: “It felt like it meant something… Now the bar has been lowered to such a degree, societally. If you can have just a person at that base level of moral decrepitude as Trump […] He’s a conman, [who] has done nothing but rob the country.”

Oberst labelled the US’ current political landscape as “absurd”, saying that writing about it was “almost impossible”.

Speaking to NME in 2024 around the release of Bright Eyes’ latest album, ‘Five Dice, All Threes’, Oberst discussed writing about politicians swearing their oaths of office on the song ‘Hate’.

“I mean, the hypocrisy of all that stuff is so palpable. I think 95 per cent of politicians are full of shit,” he told us. “I didn’t have to get a PhD to come up with that conclusion. I just happen to have read the newspaper for most of my life.”

When asked about the 2024 US election – which was coming up at the time, and ultimately saw Trump return to the White House – Oberst responded: “I mean, knock on wood, but I think that Vice President Harris would make a great president. She’s incredibly smart. She seems empathetic and very, very qualified. I don’t want to say that it’s a done deal, people need to show up, and there are some hurdles as far as being the first woman of colour [to run for president].

“For progressive leaning friends of mine it felt that when Biden was still in the race, it was like everyone had resigned to the fact that he was probably going to lose, and that it was gonna be horrible. Now it feels like we might actually be able to pull this thing off, if everyone gets together and agrees that, sadly, we have a binary option, and given the two options, one is clearly much better than the other. I hope that carries us across the finish line.”

In other news, one of Bright Eyes’ 21st anniversary shows in New York had to be evacuated due to extreme weather last month. The tour kicked off at Red Rocks in Colorado, where they played 2005’s ‘I’m Wide Awake, It’s Morning’ and ‘Digital Ash In A Digital Urn’ in full.

Bright Eyes will take to the stage at Maha Festival next month before heading to Riot Fest in September, ahead of some more US dates in the autumn.

Andy Burnham has announced that music venues, clubs and pubs in England will get a 20 per cent cut in business rates next year, and the costs will be funded by a review of the tax reliefs given to companies “such as vape shops”.

The announcement was made at 6am today (Thursday July 23) and marks the latest of multiple policy changes that Burnham has confirmed in his first few days as Prime Minister – following Keir Starmer stepping down.

With the move, pubs and music venues across the country will see tax rates decline by 20 per cent. The cut will cost around £100million, and will be paid for with higher taxes on businesses that “do not make a positive contribution to local communities, such as vape shops”.

Burnham added that the government is wanting to “crack down” on companies that are selling through online marketplaces and “do not comply with their tax obligations”, and has previously suggested that he wants to raise the threshold at which business rates kick in too – meaning that many small high-street retailers would avoid paying altogether.

The decrease in taxes is expected to save a typical UK pub around £1,100 per year, with 32,000 other businesses enjoying similar benefits, and while the new move will affect most live music spaces, it will not extend to the biggest music venues.

Government announce £45million to “turn up” music industry for artists, businesses and children – but what do Andy Burnham’s government need to do now?

📅 Jul 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The UK government has announced details of a £45million investment to “turn up” the music industry, but attention now turns on the work that needs to be done by new Prime Minister Andy Burnham and his government.

While former Mayor Of Manchester Andy Burnham officially entered Downing Street as new Labour leader and UK Prime Minister after the resignation of Keir Starmer today (Monday July 20), last week saw the government reveal their ‘Music Plan’ to bolster their growth package by £15million to a total of £45million. They say this will back over 40,000 artists and businesses, 2,000 projects and millions of children over three years. The hope is to destroy the glass ceiling that is seeing less people from working class backgrounds enter the world of music.

“Music is our shared language. It brings people together, breaks down barriers and gives voice to every community. At a time when too many people feel disconnected, music has never mattered more,” said Culture Secretary, Lisa Nandy. “As the song says, ‘Every generation throws a hero up the pop charts.’ But pop is getting posher, and that must change.

“Talent is everywhere, but opportunity is not. After a decade of mindless vandalism that stripped culture and creativity from too many classrooms and communities, we are putting music back at the heart of education, investing in creative careers and backing grassroots music across the country.”

Nandy added: “We believe music belongs to everyone, not just the privileged few. That’s how we widen opportunity, strengthen our communities and ensure the UK’s incredible fans and extraordinary musical talent continues to inspire the world for generations to come.”

View this post on Instagram

‘Turn It Up: Our Plan for Music’ aims to further boost the UK’s music industry, worth around £8billion to the economy. The cash includes £12.5million for libraries to be transformed into music lending libraries with free studio spaces and live performance opportunities and £10million for creative mentoring for young people.

Speaking to NME, the Minister for Creative Industries, Media and Arts and for Digital Government and Data Ian Murray MP said that he hoped this investment would lead to “a fundamental step change” in the UK’s creative sphere.

“The UK music industry is worth £8billion to the economy, we want to grow the sector, and there’s not another country in the world that wouldn’t give their right arm for a fraction of what we do in the UK,” said Murray. “We’ve given the soundtrack to the world before and we want to make sure that future generations can do the same. It’s about artists, it’s about the talent pipeline, it’s about venues, it’s about access. We know there’s talent everywhere but opportunity is not, and the music plan is there to try and address some of that.”

Asked what his message would be to those who feel that the world of music is out of their reach and only the playground of the wealthy, he replied: “This government is on your side.”

“This music plan is about working with the industry to make sure you can get access to it and allow your talent to flourish,” he shared. “We’re trying to get that to 40,000 musicians and artists over the next three years. I would say to any fledgling artists out there who have never picked up an instrument to pick one up. To all musicians out there, please use this plan and hold us to account for it. We want you to have access to the music industry.”

The government also shared plans to cut red tape for music festivals to allow for longer licences. “All of that bureaucratic red tape stuff has really hampered things,” said Murray of the lack of growth for UK festivals. “You have to go through it on an annual basis, and we want new events and festivals to have a freer licensing routine so they can have a bit of certainty about growing their business through their licensing, rather than just having one year of certainty. Having five year licensing will mean they can plan, grow and invest.

“We need to also make sure that Agent For Change for live music is there so that new developments can’t come in and shut down existing music venues through noise complaints. It’s about the entirety of government, local government and bureaucracy to work together for the benefit of the music industry rather than being a hindrance to it.”

Meanwhile, established and beloved music venues continue to face threats from noise complaints and new developments – with London’s legendary Moth Club currently at war with the council. Mr Murray MP said that a detailed national policy framework was due soon and that they’d be working with Music Venue Trust to ensure that councils have all the information required to “protect and save these spaces”.

New Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s love of music is well-known, with the former Mayor Of Manchester leadership bid gaining support from local legends Oasis, Courteeners, Elbow and more. He previously served as Culture Secretary from 2008-2009.

He’s also been very vocal in his support of grassroots music venues and in pushing for “urgent action” on the £1 ticket levy on gigs at arena level and above to pay back into the pipeline for rising artists and essential smaller gig spaces. He enters his premiership at a vital time for the sector.

Last year saw 30 grassroots venues lost forever with more than half of those remaining making no profit and over 6,000 jobs lost – posing an existential threat to the future talent pipeline of the country’s world-renowned live music industry.

The £1 levy collected and distributed by the LIVE Trust has received widespread support and could provide a future-proof lifeline for grassroots talent and venues. At the start of this year, a deadline was set for June 30 for 50 per cent of all gigs at arena level and above to be voluntarily paying into the scheme before the government steps in and makes in statutory and mandatory by law. It is currently believed to be around 30 per cent with the deadline now past.

With just 8.8 per cent of shows in 2025 signing up to the levy, Music Venue Trust CEO Davyd put the blame largely at the foot of market leader Live Nation.

“These companies are delivering,” he said of the likes of SJM, Kilimanjaro and AEG back in January. “Live Nation, you know, and the whole industry knows, you are not. If the voluntary levy fails, it will not be the fault of the companies who have already embraced it, or Music Venue Trust, or the government, or any will to do it on behalf of individuals, artists, managers, agents, audiences or anyone else.

“It will be a direct consequence of the overwhelmingly dominant force in the arena and stadium market deciding not to deliver a voluntary levy. That’s your choice, Live Nation, and everyone in the industry hopes you make the right one.”

At the time, Live Nation responded to the criticism by telling NME that they “support artists’ choices on charitable donations, and has worked with numerous artists who have contributed to the voluntary levy – from Coldplay and Harry Styles to Biffy Clyro – and will continue to do so.”

Speaking of the levy, Mr Murray MP described it as “huge for the music industry”.

“The LIVE Trust have just put £100,000 in, there’s another £1million on the way, all to support grassroots music, artists and venues,” he told NME. “We want to see that happening across the country so while our target was 50 per cent, I want 100 per cent of those tickets to have the £1 voluntary levy on it so we can have more money going to the grassroots. We need the big artists to be supporting the very grassroots that they’ve come through in order to make their careers successful.”

With the the deadline now passed, Murray would not speak on any imminent moves to make the levy statutory. “We want this to be voluntary because it’s for the industry to come together, but we have always said that if it isn’t on a voluntary basis then we might look to legislate for it,” he shared. “It works best when the industry comes to the conclusion that this is good for the industry.”

He added: “I would say to the big promoters to the big artists that they have to start somewhere, and those venues and places where they started are under threat and we need to support them, we need to grow them, we need to make sure there’s much more availability. Grassroots funding is all part of that, and that’s why the £1 voluntary levy is so important.”

Chief Executive of UK Music Tom Kiehl welcomed Andy Burnham into his new role, but called on him to deliver on past commitment to the industry and its artists by seeing through policies to remove costly and prohibitive barriers of the post-Brexit touring crisis,
rule out any new copyright exceptions in relation to Artificial Intelligence and deliver on the Government’s manifesto pledge to ban ticket touts.

“We welcome Andy Burnham back in Westminster as the new Prime Minister and look forward to continuing our work championing the music industry with him,” said Kiehl. “As a former Culture Secretary and big music fan, he has a deep understanding of what makes the music industry tick. He also knows the challenges facing a sector which is one of the Government’s best international calling cards with the £4.8billion we generate in annual exports.”

There was widespread disappointment back in May when the government shared news of only a draft bill to stamp out secondary ticketing in the King’s speech. Kiehl has now argued that “the new Prime Minister must speed up long-promised legislation to crack down on ticket touts who rip off millions of music fans by reselling concert and festival tickets at exorbitant prices.”

Mr Murray MP has assured NME that they’re working hard to deliver on this policy this year.

“The draft bill is being worked on as we speak,” he told NME. “We are very keen to get this done as quickly as possible, we have the commitment  and we can do it in this term. Everyone is working together to do it and we’ve done all the consultations already. We want to get this bill through, I am personally committed to it, the secretary of state is too, and it’s a great news story for fans. We need to make sure that every single penny that fans spend on tickets is going back into the industry and not into the pockets of touts.”

Back in March, the government and the EU pledged “full commitment” to solving the Brexit touring crisis for artists. Burnham himself has previously slammed the “ridiculous” post-Brexit rules and demanded “free exchange” of artists through Europe. Kiehl agreed that the time was now to “tear down the barriers of red tape and extra costs that make it so hard for UK music creators, performers and crew to tour the EU post-Brexit.”

Mr Murray MP said that they were working hard to resolve this, but it was proving to be a slow process.

“It’s very complex because it’s not just about visas, it’s about moving around merchandise, transportation,” he said. “We’re making progress. It’s slower than we want it to be, but with the annual EU-UK agreements and summits in place, hopefully we’ll be making more progress soon.”

In his first public speech as Prime Minister today, Burnham pledged his government would be “a circuit breaker for Britain” introducing the “biggest changes in 40 years”.

“In the 1980s Britain took some wrong turns,” he said. “Political power was centralised, economic power privatised, large parts of the country de-industrialised and they still haven’t recovered. Many feel as though they’re still in decline, and they don’t have the ability to turn things around.

“And that’s why we will change politics, to make it more collaborative, more about problem solving than point scoring.”

He added: “And we will take power out of here and carry it into every postcode in the land so that they can do more and in doing more, build a new economy where we put life’s essentials back under stronger public control to make them affordable to you again.”

How to get the Tickets in Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 3+4’s Airport level

The tickets are at an airline counter just to the left of the map’s starting point. Look behind the counter for the woman with her hand raised and grind in front of her to grab the tickets. Once you do, you cannot fail to land a combo, so play it safe if you’re not that confident in your skills.

How to Nosegrind around the Horn in Canada – Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 3+4

If you’re a veteran of the powerhouse franchise, Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 3+4 offers plenty of nostalgia, down to the gap-based objectives like performing a Nosegrind around the Horn in the game’s second map, Canada.

While it can seem deceptively tricky, learning how to Nosegrind around the Horn is fairly simple. The first step is finding the spot in question, and the second is hitting your trick right so the game counts it as a Nosegrind. Here’s how you can complete that objective.

That’s the grind you need to hit. Screenshot by Destructoid

How to find the Horn in Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 3+4

The Horn is the tall tree with a hole in the middle on the frozen side of the map. It’s by the circular halfpipe encircling another, but the quickest way to get there is to turn around from where you spawn.

Do a 180 turn and look for the ledges. Screenshot by Destructoid

Drop from the first ramp, then get some distance and turn around. Speed toward the ramp and jump to a wooden ledge just across from you, then hit the brakes as soon as you touch the ground. Ollie, No Comply, and Boneless (used by pressing forward once/twice before jumping) can give you a little bit of height if you’re falling short.

After jumping on the first ledge, hop to another wooden path in front of you and either grind the rail or hold the down button to break your fall. Go through the hole in the tree, then turn around and find the small rail to the left side. You must grind this rail for a few seconds and land a Nosegrind with the Around The Horn gap to complete this objective. Hit the gap, land the combo, and cross this one off your list.

How to Nosegrind the Horn in Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 3+4

Don’t botch the landing and you’re in the clear. Screenshot by Destructoid

To make sure you’re hitting the Nosegrind, jump onto the rail using the front of your board. Usually, a simple forward + grind command works, but if you’re still landing a different trick, try to hit your grind head-on.

You need to hold the Nosegrind for around three to five seconds, which isn’t a lot of time. The Around The Horn gap will pop before you hit the ledge with the traffic cones, so it’s easy to bag two objectives for the price of one and keep on unlocking the new tracks. Rio is waiting, after all.
