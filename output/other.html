How to play Globle

To play Globle, you’re first going to have to access the game’s website. To start playing, simply enter in the name of any country of your choice. You’ll then get a colour match for that country indicating how close to or far away you are from the answer.

The deeper the colour, the closer you are. After a couple of guesses you will slowly be able to narrow down the answer. A good tip is to pick larger countries that share borders with other countries. You can clear large sections of the map this way. Early on, you might also want to try varying your guesses between countries in different continents to narrow down your options.

Recycling ANSI Art: Tracing the Origins of BBS Logon Screens

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
BBS-era "door" games are preserved by the site Break Into Chat, along with their history. Now the site's actually traced the origins of several ANSI art screens in the 1980s and "how different people later transformed the original images," starting from one of the most popular bulletin board systems in St. Louis, Missouri. Slashdot reader Kirkman14 writes: "Fire Escape" was curator of the authoritative list of bulletin board systems in St. Louis's area code and sysop of the area's most popular board. Like many sysops of that time, Fire Escape enlivened her bulletin board by finding and repurposing older ANSI art. Eventually her BBS had 18 different logon screens in its rotation, many of which featured flames or mushroom clouds or made liberal use of red and yellow. Break Into Chat follows the trail: Some, maybe even most, of Fire Escape's logon screens had actually been adapted from old school "public domain" ANSI art drawn in the late 1980s and early 1990s by folks such as Michael C. Ling, Lora Ruffner (aka "Ebony Eyes") and Violet Stymacks.... Stymacks was unusually generous with her ANSI art. When she first began drawing, she combed through Fidonet node lists looking for bulletin boards with interesting or inspiring names. When she found one, she would draw an ANSI screen that fit the board's theme, then send it to the sysop. Eventually, sysops began seeking her out, too. By late 1990, she claimed that 500 bulletin boards were using her screens. Three years later, that total had tripled, according to Boardwatch . I believe it — I have located more than 1,100 of her pieces so far. She was so prolific that it would be surprising if she hadn't reused or remixed her own work over the years... [D]id they intend for other people to do so? Or would they have considered such borrowing or adapting as "ripping" or just plain "stealing"? These questions have dogged the BBS, ASCII art, and ANSI art scenes from the beginning. A freelance writer named Patrick Grote grappled with this ethical dilemma in 1990. He had begun to publish archives of ANSI art that he had collected from various public messageboards and networks. But what were people allowed to do with the ANSI screens in these collections? Was it okay to modify them? "Tough call," Grote said. "I know some people copyright their screens by inserting a little '(C)' on it," Grote wrote in a README file included in these collections. "Is this 'true' copyrighting? I would say yes, but then again, I don't feel like having any legal action taken against me for using an ANSI screen. In my opinion, I would say leave the ones that say '(C)' alone, but make every effort to contact the artist and ask for permission...." When I corresponded with Fire Escape this year, she admitted her memory wasn't what it used to be, but felt certain that she had not asked any artists for permission because she didn't know who they were or how to reach them. "I figured if anyone had an issue, I'd just remove the screen," she said. "But no one ever said anything about it."

Lily Allen has poked fun at the ticket sales controversy surrounding her recent US tour by dressing up as a Ticketmaster seating map full of unsold tickets for Halloween.

The singer shared photos of her outfit on Instagram yesterday (October 30), wearing a white puffer jacket and matching bodysuit covered in blue dots, alongside a black top marked “Stage”.

She also posed with a thermometer, referencing the term “blue dot fever”, which has been used throughout 2026 to describe artists struggling to sell tickets for their live shows.

The phrase refers to the blue dots on Ticketmaster’s interactive seating plans, which represent tickets that remain available for purchase. A proliferation of blue dots has become shorthand for disappointing ticket sales and overly ambitious venue bookings.

Allen captioned the photos: “Never beating the allegations. #bluedotfever”.

The joke follows the singer’s decision to downsize several venues on the North American leg of her ‘West End Girl’ tour earlier this year.

In July, she announced that concerts in Chicago, Los Angeles, San Francisco and other cities would be moved to smaller venues, while a planned show in Montreal was cancelled.

Among the changes was the relocation of her Chicago concert from the 23,500-capacity United Center to the 3,500-capacity Chicago Theatre. A planned show at San Francisco’s Chase Center was also moved to Oakland’s smaller Paramount Theatre.

Speaking about the decision in an interview last month, Allen acknowledged: “The ticket sales weren’t there.”

She explained that the original venue bookings had been driven by promoters’ expectations, and that she had expressed concerns about the scale of the tour from the outset.

“I said to my manager, ‘Are you sure? This seems like a stretch.’ And he was like, ‘This is what they’re telling me.’ So we accepted it,” she recalled.

Allen said she had been reluctant to cancel the concerts outright because she had a crew of around 50 people relying on the work.

She also criticised the lack of transparency in concert ticketing, recounting how fans had obtained cheap last-minute tickets for shows she had previously been told were sold out.

“I think the live industry is a minefield, and I don’t know how it works,” she said. “Someone knows, but it ain’t the artist.”

The tour has seen Allen perform her acclaimed 2025 album ‘West End Girl’ in its entirety, in a theatrical production featuring backing tracks rather than a live band, with minimal audience interaction.

She previously defended the unconventional format after facing criticism, explaining: “It’s my artistic choice not to talk to the audience, the fourth wall helps with the storytelling.”

“Everyone on this tour is really working very hard to give people the best show we possibly can, and I’m extremely proud of it.”

NME reviewed the tour at the London Palladium in March, awarding it three stars and describing the production as “a powerful retelling of one of pop’s most gut-wrenching albums”.

The album itself, inspired by the breakdown of Allen’s marriage to Stranger Things actor David Harbour, received a four-star review from NME, which hailed it as “a vicious, vulnerable and victorious comeback”.

The ultimate aim in Moon Sweepers is to create your own automated factory that makes you profit without your active participation. In simple terms, I am talking about automation and making full use of it. Let’s say the total space on the moon is limited, and an efficient design is going to make things easier.

Most efficient factory setup in Moon Sweepers

For this guide, you need to have access to all the endgame features. Until then, it’s best to do everything manually. For this setup, we will be using Capsule Catchers, Pressure Chambers, and Stone Presses. However, the aim is to maximize the efficiency of Conveyor Belts since you can connect at most 12 of them.

For our optimal setup, we will use two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers, and eight Stone Presses. However, let’s first understand how the method works by breaking down the setup.

Screenshot by Destructoid

First, place the Capsule Catcher. For maximum efficiency, you want to use four bots for a singe catcher. Next, there will be two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Place two Pressure Chambers. As I said, there are two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher: One for Dust, and one for Sand.

Put a Splitter at one of the two Conveyor Belts connected to the Capsule Catcher. Draw two more belts from the Splitter and connect each to the Pressure Chambers. Now, the Capsule Catcher will send Dust to the Pressure Chambers and produce Moon Sand. We have one of our two key ingredients.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Next, place four Stone Presses. We will connect two Stone Presses with one Pressure Chamber. To do so, put a Splitter at the end of the Conveyor Belt that connects the two.

If I name my Pressure Chambers as PC1 and PC2 (suppose), Stone Press 1 and Stone Press 2 will be connected to PC1, and Stone Press 3 and 4 will be connected to PC2. Make sure that they’re connected to the correct paths (Sand must go to the terminals that accept Sand).

Screenshot by Destructoid

Now, put Splitters at the end of the Conveyor Belts that will deliver the Dust from the Capsule Catchers. Put the first Splitter to break the path into two paths, and one more on each to further split the two paths into four.

Each of these four belt paths will be connected to the Dust terminal of your Stone Presses. Now, your Stone Presses have Sand and Dust, and they can produce Moon Stone. This has the best rate of returns in terms of profits.

Finally, use Mergers to combine the output of all four Presses into a single Conveyor Belt that will be connected to your base.

For maximum efficiency, you’ll want to do this setup with two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers and eight Stone Presses. The method remains the same, but the design is just going to be more complex.

An alternate way to do the same setup is by using the garage. This applies if you are carrying most of the dust in your vehicles. In that case, you’ll want to replace the Capsule Catches with Garages. When you unload the dust, it will kickstart the same process that’s shown in the method above. However, this method is less optimum in my books as it isn’t fully automated.

Fanmade Star Wars game 19 years in the making is finally here, and it’s based on the greatest space sim ever made

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

FreeSpace Open project Fate of the Galaxy, a Star Wars-based total conversion of the classic space sim FreeSpace 2, has finally made it out of the modding hangars, having first started development in 2007.

Now, if you were ever involved in modding circles, you know how many ambitious projects can sprout in a community, many of which will never make the finish line—and the bigger the project and the longer the development cycle grows, life inevitably tends to get in the way. So the folks behind Fate of the Galaxy deserve a massive, massive round of applause for getting this past the finish line.

To underline how ridiculous this timeline is: FreeSpace 2 itself was released eight years prior, and Fate of the Galaxy was not even the first attempt at making such a mod for the game. (I see you, Star Wars Conversion Project! I was there!)

As the total conversion’s page explains, the “goal is to deliver starfighter combat that captures the look and feel of the movies.” It certainly got the look and the sound right, based on the trailer:

Eagle-eyed readers are likely flabbergasted by the fact that this nifty little thing is running on the engine of a space sim first released in 1999. I will for sure revisit the story of this incredible title and the community that it sparked someday, but the short version is that Volition made what is still considered to be the best game of its kind, but Interplay’s poor marketing decisions led to poor sales, the developers were spun off to THQ while they kept the franchise rights, and their subsequent financial collapse left the series in limbo, with studio itself closing its doors in 2023.

However, the game’s source code was released for noncommercial use, and the FreeSpace Open project that emerged from the ashes of a fantastic game that would never get another sequel has kept polishing and upgrading the underlying engine, producing absolutely incredible content to this day. The total conversion is completely free, ready for you to take to the stars after just a few clicks on its ModDB page: you don’t even need a copy of FreeSpace 2 itself, though I would strenuously recommend that you give that a try too. It’s a treasure trove of great sci-fi.

I don’t think we can ever have enough Star Wars space games, and Fate of the Galaxy is filling a surprisingly large hole. I never really got the hang of Star Wars: SQUADRONS, precisely because it didn’t have the  FreeSpace physics I grew up with, so I’m going to be all over this one once I have a bit of free time.

Poignantly, the work is dedicated to a team member who “tragically passed away in 2023:” to my shock, it was a fellow traveler—and fellow countryman, even—from my own FreeSpace modding days, and we even worked together on a campaign-that-ballooned-into-something-like-a-TC for a long while.

So I really hope the Force is strong with this one.
