Concern that the speed and scale of future warfare could be too great for humans to handle without help is growing; however, Army officials have said artificial intelligence can help soldiers process large amounts of data from sensors and networks to deliver potential responses in a fight.

At the Association of the United States Army convention in Washington, DC, last month, service leaders and soldiers spoke about using AI to process battlefield data and help troops make decisions without removing humans from the process.

"I think it's important to use super intelligence… for things that humans aren't necessarily good at, and that's speed and scale," Brig. Gen. Steven Carpenter, head of Multi-Domain Command Europe, told Business Insider, "but I also know we shouldn't take humans completely out of the loop."

That volume of targets and threats could overwhelm human analysts, but AI could help them keep up.

In a conflict, soldiers could use AI to analyze battlefield data and targets based on a commander's objectives and other parameters. The system could then assess available military capabilities and recommend responses, from conventional weapons strikes to electronic warfare or cyber operations.

"So again, the human is making the decision, the final call, looking at everything that you need to do in order to prosecute a valid target within the Law of Armed Conflict," Carpenter said.

Carpenter said that the estimates for targets and threats reflect "what we think Ukraine's worst day is," underscoring the scale of attacks US forces could face in a future conflict. Russia's war against Ukraine has involved frequent, heavy drone and missile bombardments. These strike packages are complex, consisting of ballistic and cruise missiles, as well as one-way attack drones, more and more of which are equipped with jet engines that make them faster and harder to kill.

Earlier this year, a joint US Army and NATO exercise in Europe saw troops use a homegrown AI system to consume and sort data. The AI was in beta-testing; Army officials said they aimed to test it on a larger scale at a future exercise.

The integration of AI across the military is moving fast, spanning from autonomous features in drones, weapons, and targeting to agentic processes for sorting through data and referencing information. Beyond the battlefield, US personnel are using new tools to change institutional processes and workflows.

The Army is also incorporating AI into its Next Generation Command and Control, the service's new warfighting system. NGC2 uses AI and machine learning to provide commanders and soldiers with real-time data on targeting, capabilities, ammunition levels, maintenance needs, intelligence feeds, and simulated enemy attacks.

Flying across several time zones often causes jet lag, a feeling when the body insists it's the middle of the night while the clock says it's lunchtime. Now it turns out this nuisance is not exclusive to humans.

A new Science study on Manx shearwaters, seabirds that can fly hundreds of kilometers a day over the North Atlantic, shows they jet-lag themselves during their excursions. And because these birds navigate by the Sun, that jet lag leaves a measurable mark—it skews their compass.

Clockwork compass

Patrick Lewin is a behavioral and sensory ecologist at the University of Oxford and lead author of the study. “Manx shearwaters are medium-sized birds, about the same size as a pigeon,” Lewin said. “They are white underneath and black on top, but they have very long wings, and they are closely related to albatross.” Manx shearwaters nest in burrows on coastal islands around the North Atlantic. Most breed around the UK and Ireland, with about half of their population living in Wales.

She also posed with a thermometer, referencing the term “blue dot fever”, which has been used throughout 2026 to describe artists struggling to sell tickets for their live shows.

The phrase refers to the blue dots on Ticketmaster’s interactive seating plans, which represent tickets that remain available for purchase. A proliferation of blue dots has become shorthand for disappointing ticket sales and overly ambitious venue bookings.

The joke follows the singer’s decision to downsize several venues on the North American leg of her ‘West End Girl’ tour earlier this year.

In July, she announced that concerts in Chicago, Los Angeles, San Francisco and other cities would be moved to smaller venues, while a planned show in Montreal was cancelled.

Among the changes was the relocation of her Chicago concert from the 23,500-capacity United Center to the 3,500-capacity Chicago Theatre. A planned show at San Francisco’s Chase Center was also moved to Oakland’s smaller Paramount Theatre.

Speaking about the decision in an interview last month, Allen acknowledged: “The ticket sales weren’t there.”

She explained that the original venue bookings had been driven by promoters’ expectations, and that she had expressed concerns about the scale of the tour from the outset.

“I said to my manager, ‘Are you sure? This seems like a stretch.’ And he was like, ‘This is what they’re telling me.’ So we accepted it,” she recalled.

She also criticised the lack of transparency in concert ticketing, recounting how fans had obtained cheap last-minute tickets for shows she had previously been told were sold out.

“I think the live industry is a minefield, and I don’t know how it works,” she said. “Someone knows, but it ain’t the artist.”

The tour has seen Allen perform her acclaimed 2025 album ‘West End Girl’ in its entirety, in a theatrical production featuring backing tracks rather than a live band, with minimal audience interaction.

She previously defended the unconventional format after facing criticism, explaining: “It’s my artistic choice not to talk to the audience, the fourth wall helps with the storytelling.”

“Everyone on this tour is really working very hard to give people the best show we possibly can, and I’m extremely proud of it.”

NME reviewed the tour at the London Palladium in March, awarding it three stars and describing the production as “a powerful retelling of one of pop’s most gut-wrenching albums”.

The album itself, inspired by the breakdown of Allen’s marriage to Stranger Things actor David Harbour, received a four-star review from NME, which hailed it as “a vicious, vulnerable and victorious comeback”.
