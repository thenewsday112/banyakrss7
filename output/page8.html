This Martian meteorite just got dubbed a Rosetta Stone. Heres why.

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One meteorite from Mars adds weight to the expression "no stone left unturned." 

Yang Liu, a geologist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, almost didn't heed that wisdom when she nearly declined the chance to study the rock. It looked like the most common type of Martian meteorite found on Earth. But something about its texture and minerals made her take a look.

In hindsight, Liu is glad she did. The space rock, known as Teghaza 001, appears to be a piece of Mars that formed more than 4.1 billion years ago, making it one of the oldest Martian meteorites scientists have ever seen.

"This meteorite is really unique," said Liu, the lead author of the findings, in a statement. "One of the paper's reviewers called it a Rosetta Stone for Mars."

The meteorite, found in the Sahara Desert in 2022, preserves a snapshot of Mars as it looked more than 4 billion years ago, perhaps less than 500 million years after the planet formed. Even then, the water locked inside its minerals had already lost much of its lighter hydrogen to space. That chemical fingerprint shows the Red Planet began losing its water almost from the word "go." The discovery provides context for any debate about life on early Mars: Whatever biology that might have existed, even early on, probably faced a world already drying out.

Every day, about 48.5 tons of space rock and dust strike Earth's atmosphere, according to NASA. Most of it burns up on the way down, and much of what survives sinks into the oceans, which cover more than 70 percent of the planet. People have found more than 82,000 meteorites, but only about half of one percent of them came from Mars.

Nearly every known Martian meteorite formed from magma, or molten rock, and most of them belong to three main families: shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites. All three formed less than 2.4 billion years ago. 

A close-up look at a slice extracted from the Martian meteorite, Teghaza 001. Credit: NASA / JPL-Caltech

The nakhlites and chassignites seem closely related through a process called fractional crystallization. As magma cools, certain minerals harden first and separate out, which changes the chemistry of the remaining liquid. Scientists think both groups formed in the same volcanic system.

But Teghaza 001 doesn't fit any of these groups. Its minerals and chemistry set it apart from every other Martian meteorite. The study appears in the journal Science.

"This is really our first piece of Martian crust from a part of Mars we've never had a sample of before," said Lee Saper, a co-author now based at NASA's Ames Research Center in California.

Clues in the rock's minerals point to a busy water cycle on early Mars, with water moving between rock, ground, and air. The minerals reveal two sources: water that rose from deep inside the planet, and surface water that had already lost much of its lighter hydrogen to space. 

Scientists use geological clues to piece together ancient Mars' past water history. Credit: NASA illustration

Geologists can tell the two apart because hydrogen comes in a normal form and a heavier form called deuterium. Because the lighter form escapes into space more easily, any water left behind gets richer in deuterium over time. The surface water in Teghaza 001 carries extra deuterium.

From about 4.1 to 3.7 billion years ago, Mars had a fairly dry climate, but one that could have still allowed liquid water on the surface at times, possibly enabling life. Scientists believe the Red Planet's surface eventually dried out to roughly today's barren state less than 3 billion years ago.

Thanks to Mars' lack of shifting tectonic plates, its ground maintains a record of more than 4 billion years of history. That allows scientists to estimate the ages of different parts of the planet's surface by counting craters: the older the region, the more battle scars it should have. 

Based on the specimen's minerals and chemistry, scientists trace the rock to one of a handful of locations in Mars' southern highlands. Credit: NASA / JPL-Caltech / USGS

The mix of minerals matches what NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has seen in the heavily pocked southern highlands of Mars. Scientists pinpointed 13 places on Mars whose minerals and chemistry are consistent with the meteorite.

To date Teghaza 001, scientists used several methods, including one cutting-edge technique that tracks radioactive rubidium, said Francois Tissot, a Caltech scientist and co-author. The rubidium slowly transforms into strontium at a steady rate; thus, the amount of the latter within a sample stands in for a clock. 

"Our hope is to pave the way for dating even more Martian meteorites," Tissot said in a statement, "or samples returned from other planets, their moons, and asteroids."

The ultimate aim in Moon Sweepers is to create your own automated factory that makes you profit without your active participation. In simple terms, I am talking about automation and making full use of it. Let’s say the total space on the moon is limited, and an efficient design is going to make things easier.

Most efficient factory setup in Moon Sweepers

For this guide, you need to have access to all the endgame features. Until then, it’s best to do everything manually. For this setup, we will be using Capsule Catchers, Pressure Chambers, and Stone Presses. However, the aim is to maximize the efficiency of Conveyor Belts since you can connect at most 12 of them.

For our optimal setup, we will use two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers, and eight Stone Presses. However, let’s first understand how the method works by breaking down the setup.

Screenshot by Destructoid

First, place the Capsule Catcher. For maximum efficiency, you want to use four bots for a singe catcher. Next, there will be two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Place two Pressure Chambers. As I said, there are two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher: One for Dust, and one for Sand.

Put a Splitter at one of the two Conveyor Belts connected to the Capsule Catcher. Draw two more belts from the Splitter and connect each to the Pressure Chambers. Now, the Capsule Catcher will send Dust to the Pressure Chambers and produce Moon Sand. We have one of our two key ingredients.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Next, place four Stone Presses. We will connect two Stone Presses with one Pressure Chamber. To do so, put a Splitter at the end of the Conveyor Belt that connects the two.

If I name my Pressure Chambers as PC1 and PC2 (suppose), Stone Press 1 and Stone Press 2 will be connected to PC1, and Stone Press 3 and 4 will be connected to PC2. Make sure that they’re connected to the correct paths (Sand must go to the terminals that accept Sand).

Screenshot by Destructoid

Now, put Splitters at the end of the Conveyor Belts that will deliver the Dust from the Capsule Catchers. Put the first Splitter to break the path into two paths, and one more on each to further split the two paths into four.

Each of these four belt paths will be connected to the Dust terminal of your Stone Presses. Now, your Stone Presses have Sand and Dust, and they can produce Moon Stone. This has the best rate of returns in terms of profits.

Finally, use Mergers to combine the output of all four Presses into a single Conveyor Belt that will be connected to your base.

For maximum efficiency, you’ll want to do this setup with two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers and eight Stone Presses. The method remains the same, but the design is just going to be more complex.

An alternate way to do the same setup is by using the garage. This applies if you are carrying most of the dust in your vehicles. In that case, you’ll want to replace the Capsule Catches with Garages. When you unload the dust, it will kickstart the same process that’s shown in the method above. However, this method is less optimum in my books as it isn’t fully automated.

Moon phase today: What the Moon will look like on October 31, 2026

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Moon is still looking fairly full in the night sky, but we're now in the Waning Gibbous phase, when the illuminated portion starts to decrease night by night.

What is today's Moon phase?

As of Saturday, Oct. 31, NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Gibbous phase, with 68% of its surface visible.

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Tycho Crater, Copernicus Crater, and the Mare Imbrium. Binoculars will also help you see the Alphonsus Crater, the Clavius Crater, and the Apennine Mountains. And, if you have access to a telescope, you'll also see the Apollo 16 landing spot, the Schiller Crater, and the Rima Ariadaeus.

When is the next Full Moon?

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Once again, NASA is asking for ideas to rescue Hubble from certain death

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Hubble Space Telescope still shines in the eyes of astronomers after 36 years in orbit, but it is not as nimble as it once was. Left alone, the observatory will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s, closing the book on one of the most famous scientific instruments of the century.

NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission. The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope's orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction.

The space agency released a Request For Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble. Responses are due November 30. Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies' current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope's performance, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry.

Fanmade Star Wars game 19 years in the making is finally here, and it’s based on the greatest space sim ever made

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

FreeSpace Open project Fate of the Galaxy, a Star Wars-based total conversion of the classic space sim FreeSpace 2, has finally made it out of the modding hangars, having first started development in 2007.

Now, if you were ever involved in modding circles, you know how many ambitious projects can sprout in a community, many of which will never make the finish line—and the bigger the project and the longer the development cycle grows, life inevitably tends to get in the way. So the folks behind Fate of the Galaxy deserve a massive, massive round of applause for getting this past the finish line.

To underline how ridiculous this timeline is: FreeSpace 2 itself was released eight years prior, and Fate of the Galaxy was not even the first attempt at making such a mod for the game. (I see you, Star Wars Conversion Project! I was there!)

As the total conversion’s page explains, the “goal is to deliver starfighter combat that captures the look and feel of the movies.” It certainly got the look and the sound right, based on the trailer:

Eagle-eyed readers are likely flabbergasted by the fact that this nifty little thing is running on the engine of a space sim first released in 1999. I will for sure revisit the story of this incredible title and the community that it sparked someday, but the short version is that Volition made what is still considered to be the best game of its kind, but Interplay’s poor marketing decisions led to poor sales, the developers were spun off to THQ while they kept the franchise rights, and their subsequent financial collapse left the series in limbo, with studio itself closing its doors in 2023.

However, the game’s source code was released for noncommercial use, and the FreeSpace Open project that emerged from the ashes of a fantastic game that would never get another sequel has kept polishing and upgrading the underlying engine, producing absolutely incredible content to this day. The total conversion is completely free, ready for you to take to the stars after just a few clicks on its ModDB page: you don’t even need a copy of FreeSpace 2 itself, though I would strenuously recommend that you give that a try too. It’s a treasure trove of great sci-fi.

I don’t think we can ever have enough Star Wars space games, and Fate of the Galaxy is filling a surprisingly large hole. I never really got the hang of Star Wars: SQUADRONS, precisely because it didn’t have the  FreeSpace physics I grew up with, so I’m going to be all over this one once I have a bit of free time.

Poignantly, the work is dedicated to a team member who “tragically passed away in 2023:” to my shock, it was a fellow traveler—and fellow countryman, even—from my own FreeSpace modding days, and we even worked together on a campaign-that-ballooned-into-something-like-a-TC for a long while.

So I really hope the Force is strong with this one.
