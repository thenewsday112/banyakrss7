Wunderhorse guitarist Harry Fowler has surprise released his debut solo album ‘Blankets’, alongside the single ‘Perfect Colours’ – listen below.

  • READ MORE: How Wunderhorse banded together: “You’ve got to look out for each other. It’s a cut-throat industry”

The record arrived today (October 30) without any prior announcement, marking Fowler’s first solo release outside of his role with the band.

Described as an intimate collection of songs that the guitarist has accumulated since his teenage years, ‘Blankets’ takes a stripped-back approach, combining sparse, finger-picked acoustic guitar with delicate vocals.

The album has been released independently and is available to stream here. Fowler has also made a limited run of just 300 vinyl copies available through his official website.

The release is accompanied by the debut single ‘Perfect Colours’, which showcases the more understated direction of his solo material, driven softly by lilting piano notes and an aching lead vocal.

Listen to the track below:

Fowler is best known as the guitarist in Wunderhorse, the band fronted by Jacob Slater, who began the project as a solo venture before expanding it into a full group.

The two musicians have been friends since they were 14, having bonded over their shared love of Led Zeppelin, Pearl Jam and Nirvana while attending school in Hertfordshire, as they recalled in a 2024 interview with NME.

Wunderhorse released their second album ‘Midas’, their first as a fully established four-piece, that same year, with Fowler featuring alongside Slater, drummer Jamie Staples and then-bassist Peter Woodin.

The album followed 2022’s ‘Cub’, which had originally been conceived as Slater’s solo project.

Reviewing their show at Glastonbury 2025, NME wrote: “The London band meet those high expectations head-on with a set that’s searing and masterful, showcasing the best of their discography so far and what makes them such a vital act.”

Wunderhorse have continued working on new material throughout 2026, and debuted two unreleased songs, ‘Kerosene’ and ‘Promises’, during their North American tour in July.

Speaking to NME at Glastonbury last year, Slater suggested that the band’s next record could take a more optimistic direction.

“I’m trying to maybe write something a little more hopeful these days,” he explained. “‘Midas’ was pretty bleak, so maybe that’s the bridge to whatever we’re gonna do next.”

Meanwhile, Wunderhorse are set to support Sam Fender on his huge 2027 UK stadium tour, including dates at Newcastle’s St James’ Park, Wembley Stadium and Manchester’s Etihad Stadium.

Fender announced three additional dates earlier today following overwhelming demand for tickets, with Wunderhorse supporting at the new Cardiff and Edinburgh shows.

There’s no debating that many factors went into BioWare’s closure, but the biggest has to be the fact that the studio hasn’t made a game reflective of its past successes in quite some time. As the conversation around the impact of AI on music and creatives grows ever louder, industry leaders have laid out what needs to be done to protect artists and the UK’s creative forces in the future.

  • READ MORE: NME’s top 10 shows from Beyond The Music 2026

This month saw a gathering of industry leaders, artists and experts at Manchester’s Beyond The Music Festival, where an AI summit was held come up with a set of principles based on the idea that “art is a human right” and that creators naturally have a moral and material claim to be both credited and paid for their work.

The ‘Manchester Principles’ were designed as a first-of-its-kind industry-wide plan and rights-based roadmap to align creators, rights-holders, tech platforms, AI labs and policymakers on generative AI regulations.

“When we talk about what the Manchester Principles mean and why you’re here, it’s thinking about how we start that conversation,” Mayor of Greater Manchester Bev Craig told the audience. “How does economic change and technological progress lead into social change? What are the things that we need to demand that can make our world better, safer and protect creativity and our very soul?”

Mayor Of Greater Manchester Bev Craig speaks at Beyond The Music, 206. Credit: Marieke Macklon

The key ‘Manchester Principles’ objectives agreed upon by the working group were:

  • Licensing and consent: “Establishing minimum conditions and clear frameworks for when and how human-created works can be used to train generative AI models.”
  • Provenance and tracking: “Protecting and tracking the origin of creative works and data usage.”
  • Attribution and equity: “Ensuring proper human credit and economic compensation for artists and creators whose work influences or feeds into AI-generated output.”

During a day that laid out the potential opportunities and threats of AI, the conference heard how we’re “in the first five minutes of the next industrial revolution” and that UK-based-and-owned sovereign models could harness the country’s knowledge, reward our creators and lead the way internationally for AI to be adopted in a fair and moral way.”

Introducing the talks, Ian Murray MP, Minister for Creative Industries, said that this was a sensitive area given that “66 per cent of music creators believe AI poses a direct threat”.

He added that “we cannot ask our creators to choose between embracing innovation and protecting their rights,” while we should simultaneously be working towards the UK becoming “the safest place to develop and adopt AI technology in the world”.

Clive Dickens, who founded Absolute Radio and is now founder and CEO of The Meliora Company, is helping companies and creative outlets to adapt to the ever-enveloping world of AI. Speaking at Beyond The Music, Dickens argued that “the UK must own more than the playlist” when it comes to AI and music.

“We very much believe that human creativity is enhanced by technology,” he argued, sharing that artificial intelligence today is used by just under 2billion people every week in a form “that we know didn’t really exist 50 months ago.”

“Artificial intelligence is a basic human right, in the same way that water, shelter and power are,” he continued. “If you start to think of it that way and all that underpins it, you start to think of the impact that AI will have on 8.2billion people.”

Dickens said that the industry and UK government had to quicken the pace when it came to issues of “regulation, trust and guardrails”, suggesting that they adopt Meliora’s concept or reimagining the term ‘GPT’ (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) as ‘Fair GPT’ – meaning future models would be ‘fair governed, fair powered, and fair trained.

Clive Dickens speaks at Beyond The Music, 2026. Credit: Marieke Macklon

“AI should be fair-governed,” he began. “It should have regulations and laws that help it operate fairly. We should be looking at mandatory reach reporting across our societies. We need a treaty of non-proliferation around bad AI.

“You can’t release medicine into society without regulatory approval, and you shouldn’t be allowed to release AI models without pre-approval as well.”

For the ‘fair powered’ ideal, Dickens said that more should be done to use renewable forms of energy to power data centres. As for ‘fair training’, he called for work to not be scraped or stolen, with provenance, consent and remuneration of sources mandatory. He added that with a sovereign model of its own, the UK could lead the way in future-proof AI use that’s good for humanity and protects creatives.

“People say we’re too late,” he said. “It’s not too late: we are in the first five minutes of the next industrial revolution. We can train our own model.”

Giving said model the hypothetical name ‘Hope V.1’, Dickens argued that it could also be trained on the UK’s vast wealth of non-digitised knowledge, and set for the challenge for it to be created by November 2027 when Manchester hosts the next G20 summit.

He ended by imagining “a model that is fair governed, fair powered and fair trained, where creators get paid off the back of the creative economy, allowing everyone who contributes to that economy to generate income from.”

Dickens added: “We can go one step further and have our own sovereign model, then once we have our own electricity, we can control how that creative culture is exported in our country and across the world.”

Andrew Melchoir speaks at Beyopnd The Music, 2026. Credit: Patrick Gunning

Outside of these models, tech is currently being produced to put power back in the hands of artists and creators. As well as working as chief technical officer for Massive Attack across their ambitious live productions and eco-friendly initiatives, Andrew Melchior is also CEO of Genotone. His company are working on technology that will one day prove the human origin and authorship of all recorded music.

Speaking on a panel at Beyond The Music, Melchoir said that the industry urgently needed to provide “clarity, standards and standardised tools that avoid bespoke walled gardens”.

Genotone are working on “inaudible provenance markers” and watermarks inside all audio files, so that all music can either be told apart from AI-slop or that the creators behind the original source can be traced. “My company’s goal is to try and find the Holy Grail of being able to embed artist rights at the point of creation of a piece of music that travels through the system with it,” he said.

“If we have such a standard, which could also become a public infrastructure, then we’ll have a source of truth that people can understand and machine-read at scale to quickly understand what people have opted in for or opted out of to in essence create a birth certificate for the media that you’re creating.”

The same panel saw UK Music CEO Tom Kiehl explain how issues around “mass-scraping, consent, deep-fakes and AI slop were “huge” for the industry, and “deeply personal to artists”.

“This is about how your work can be manipulated, how your work can be changed, how your work can be attributed in a different way,” said Kiehl. “This is an evolving area that we need to get right, but the music industry is an ecosystem too. It’s on all of us who play a part in the sector to make sure that people get that fair reward.”

Dua Lipa and Sir Elton John at the Elton John AIDS Foundation Academy Awards Viewing Party on April 25, 2021. (Photo by David M. Benett/Getty Images for the Elton John AIDS Foundation)

Expanding on the idea of loss of control, Baroness Beeban Kidron gave an impassioned speech about the nature of artistry. Kidron made headlines last year and gained support from the likes of Elton John, Paul McCartney and Dua Lipa for fighting against Keir Starmer’s government by introducing amendments to the UK’s Data (Use and Access) Bill in the House of Lords – calling for AI companies to transparently disclose the use of copyright-protected works and attempting to stamp out unlicensed scraping.

At Beyond The Music, the former film director said that music and art needed special protection from AI due to the emotion and intention behind the work, and what it means to fans.

“When we read a book, watch a film, listen to a piece of music or look at a painting, someone somewhere, the creator wanted us to understand something, they wanted to say something, someone made a choice, someone wanted us to hear or feel something we otherwise might not have heard or felt,” she told the audience. “It is that art of human creation (the intention, the choice, the meaning) that is stripped of value in this race to control artificial intelligence.”

She continued: “The threat is existential. Lose copyright and you lose the means of earning a living, lose control of your work and you lose control of its meaning.”

Kidron said that the pillars of “ownership and consent, transparency and traceability, fair licence and new markets” needed to be at the centre of any potential future for AI use in music, and that those within the arts needed to agree on the best route or have the future laid out for them by billionaires and tech companies.

“The danger is that if we don’t have an agreement about what we want, then there are people out there who definitely know what they don’t want us to have,” she argued. “This fight is not just about whether artists get paid. It’s actually about the value of human work, where the benefits fall, what kind of society we want to live in and who has the power to decide.”

“The labour of writers, artists, musicians stolen without permission, without payment, the raw material of human creativity, our stories, music, images and ideas harvested, repackaged and sold back to us by those who claim to be inventing the future. These are not faceless data sets, they are the cultural, emotional and intellectual work of millions of people.”

Echoing the call for sovereignty with AI models, Kidron said that this would enable the UK to make and enforce its own laws as well as allowing Britain “to pursue its national interests without coercion or dependence” from huge corporations. “As the power of the technology companies has grown, so too has their determination to shape our laws and negotiate the terms on which they will comply, and enlist the support of their own government to advance their interests,” she argued.

Baroness Beeban Kidron speaks at Beyond The Music, 2026. Credit: Patrick Gunning

Mentioning the work she’d done with young people as a leading voice on children’s rights in the digital environment, Kidron said that was shocked and saddened to hear one teen boy telling her that “the future is disappearing and he could no longer see himself in it”. Ultimately, the UK needs to get a grip of AI to safeguard the viability of creativity in decades to come.

“Hold the next generation in mind, because already the prospect of human replacement is making children lose sight of their future and there are few things more devastating than stealing a generation’s capacity to imagine a future,” added Kidron. “This is not only about what we can imagine, but also what we are prepared to determine.”

She continued: “The tech sector lobbyists are masters of inevitability, that technology moves too fast to be governed, that the price of innovation is our exploitation. But these are choices, not destinies. The choices that we make now will determine whether the next generation can see a place for themselves in our collective future. The future hasn’t disappeared – it’s being made right now. And as every creator knows, what is made can be made differently.

“Sovereignty has never meant doing everything ourselves, it has always meant retaining the freedom to decide for ourselves: who gets to tell the story of our future?”

Concluding the day’s findings, summit chair and specialist media and entertainment lawyer Cliff Fluet said that the UK music industry and its artists were looking for “creative sovereignty” as “our culture is a shared common wealth and it’s quietly wearing away.”

Fluet ended by saying that we need to put “human creativity first” ensuring that we have “consent, fair pay, transparency, and enforcement that works for everyone” or have our creative industries and artists of the past, present and future pillaged. “Whatever we build has to feed back into the society it’s built from, rather than taking from it.”

A study this summer revealed that AI was detected in 40 per cent of all music released in July alone. Jason Grishkoff, founder of SubmitHub who were behind the study, explained: “I think the path forward for AI music is disclosure. People should be able to decide for themselves whether they want to engage with AI-generated music.”

Another study in 2025 found that 97 per cent of people “can’t tell the difference” between real and AI music. The previous year, it was warned that people working in music were likely to lose a quarter of their income to AI over the next four years.

In March, figures from the UK music industry responded to new government action on copyright, which prevents AI firms from using existing work without permission. However, they argued that much more needed to be done beyond that first step.

NASAs Voyager 1 gives mission control another moment of panic

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA's farthest spacecraft gave mission control a scare when it inexplicably went silent and missed two days of planned check-ins. 

The nail-biting incident happened just two weeks ago, when the Deep Space Network didn't receive a call from Voyager 1 on either Oct. 18 or 19. But before the agency could send repair commands, the spacecraft, now outside the solar system, signaled back on Oct. 20. No communication outages have happened since. 

Engineers still don't know what caused the problem. So far, the team has discovered that one set of thrusters shut down, leaving a backup set to take over. The thrusters are small engines that point and steady the spacecraft. Voyager 1's computers do this automatically whenever they detect an issue with the active thrusters to ensure the spacecraft is in a safe mode. 

"This is the first time we've seen the spacecraft do something like this, but we are also at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected," said Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, in a statement. "We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened."

Voyager 1's trouble adds to a growing list of signs that NASA's longest-running mission is winding down. Its plutonium power source loses about four watts every year, forcing engineers to turn off two science instruments to conserve power. On Nov. 18, the spacecraft is expected to reach a historic milestone of one "light-day" from Earth. That distance, roughly 16.09 billion miles away, measures how far light travels over 24 hours.

SEE ALSO: Scientists think they've just discovered a new dwarf planet like Pluto

Voyager 1 has traveled farther than any other human-made object and is racing through space at 38,000 mph.

Launched in 1977 to survey the outer planets, the spacecraft and its twin Voyager 2 have explored four planets — Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune, and Uranus — 48 moons, and a host of planetary magnetic fields and rings.

In August 2012, Voyager 1 was the first to enter interstellar space. The two remain the only spacecraft ever to leave the solar system, though New Horizons is trying to make its escape as well.  

Both Voyagers run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which are power units that turn heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. In September, NASA engineers stretched their shrinking power supplies by turning off three instruments on each and replacing them with three lower-powered alternatives — a plan they nicknamed "the Big Bang." The devices involved in the swap also help keep the spacecraft warm enough to work in the freezing cold of space, so the swaps had to happen at the same moment to spread heat evenly.

That strategy should keep their Cosmic Ray Subsystems, Magnetometers, and Plasma Wave Subsystems running for at least one more year, according to NASA. The devices measure high‑energy particles, the local magnetic field, and ripples in charged gas around the spacecraft.

Once Voyager 1 passes one light day, a radio message from Earth will take 24 hours to reach it, and its reply will take another 24 hours to come back.

"If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response," said Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager.

Moon phase today: What the Moon will look like on October 30, 2026

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Moon is starting to look a little less full as we move through the Waning Gibbous phase, with the Third Quarter Moon coming up next in the lunar calendar.

What is today's Moon phase?

As of Friday, Oct. 30, NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Gibbous phase, with 78% of its surface visible.

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Tycho Crater, Copernicus Crater, and the Mare Serenitatis. Binoculars will also help you see the Alphonsus Crater, the Clavius Crater, and the Apennine Mountains. And, if you have access to a telescope, you'll also see the Apollo 14 landing spot, the Schiller Crater, and the Caucasus Mountains.

When is the next Full Moon?

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Scientists think theyve just discovered a new dwarf planet like Pluto

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have just spotted a small, bright world in the outer reaches of the solar system they think could be a dwarf planet like Pluto. 

The mysterious space object, for now known as 2026 ED138, lies about 46 times farther from the sun than Earth, and may measure between 370 to 930 miles wide. Scientists estimate it's about 1.1 billion miles farther than Pluto in the Kuiper Belt, a zone of icy debris past Neptune.

Matthew Holman, a researcher at the Harvard & Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, made the discovery two weeks ago by running data from the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory through a program he built with fellow astronomer Kevin Napier. 

"My first reaction was one of excited disbelief," Holman said in a statement. "Objects like this are very rare, so I immediately started checking whether I or the code had done something wrong."

The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center, the official recordkeeper for new objects in our solar system, announced it as a candidate on Wednesday. If confirmed, ED138 could become the sixth officially recognized dwarf planet in the solar system, following Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. 

SEE ALSO: Life ingredients probably didn't ride in on a comet, new study shows

Rubin's detection of a previously unknown world in our own cosmic backyard highlights a new accelerated way of doing astronomy. After the initial find, the team was able to piece together a detailed "picture" of the object in three days. By pairing the observatory's repeated sky scans with archival data and pictures from other telescopes, the researchers learned quite a bit about ED138 and its stretched orbit around the sun. 

You can think of a dwarf planet as a scaled-down planet. It gets pulled around the sun by its own gravity, but it's not quite massive enough to clear other space rocks out of its way like a regular planet does. Pluto's 2006 demotion from planet status occurred because other icy bodies cross its orbit. 

"Once we identify a new object, we can search back months, and sometimes even decades, finding it in images where nobody knew to look," said Mario Jurić, Rubin's lead solar system scientist at the University of Washington, in a statement. "This ability to both discover and 'precover' objects is transforming how quickly and accurately we can determine their orbits and understand the distant solar system."

Astronomers say ED138 shines so brightly that folks with fairly small telescopes could probably see it. The reason no one has noticed it until now may be that it hides in plain sight among the Milky Way's crowded band of stars in the sky.

Looking back in the vault, Rubin's Simonyi Survey Telescope had observed ED138 some 30 times between March 4 and July 11. Then, using the Asteroid Institute's ADAM Precovery tool, which combs archives for old matches to newly found objects, the team saw it again in 2016 images taken with the Dark Energy Camera at another observatory in Chile. Combined, the data allowed astronomers to trace the object's path over a decade. 

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory took these images of 2026 ED138, a possible dwarf planet beyond Pluto that may be one of the largest and brightest objects ever found that far from the sun. Credit: NSF–DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory / NOIRLab / SLAC / AURA / M. Jurić / D. de Martin / M. Zamani

For now, scientists call ED138 a dwarf planet candidate because they still need to prove it's round. Icy objects of this size usually get pulled into a round shape by their own gravity. To be sure, though, scientists need to observe it with other telescopes in infrared light — a kind of light our eyes can't see — to pin down its exact size and other details.

Distant objects like ED138 are artifacts from the era when the planets, including Earth, formed, and they could help explain how the solar system and its giant planets came together.

Over the next 10 years, Rubin will photograph the whole southern sky repeatedly. Scientists believe this new detection is just the beginning of many discoveries to come. 

"Any others hiding in the places earlier surveys skipped are now within reach," Holman said. 

Moon phase today: What the Moon will look like on October 29, 2026

The full Moon may be behind us, but there's still plenty of lunar activity to look forward to as October draws to a close.

What is today's Moon phase?

As of Thursday, Oct. 29, NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Gibbous phase, with 87% of its surface visible.

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Tycho Crater, Copernicus Crater, and the Mare Imbrium. Binoculars will also help you see the Posidonius Crater, the Mare Humorum, and the Grimaldi Basin. Plus, if you have a telescope, you'll also see the Apollo 12 landing spot, the Rupes Altai, and the Caucasus Mountains.

When is the next Full Moon?

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.
